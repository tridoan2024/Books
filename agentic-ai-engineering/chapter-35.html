Chapter 35: Case Study — The Research Loop

Level: S (Standard) · ~4,000 words · Levels served: L3, L4, L5

35.1 Research as a Verification Problem

A coding loop verifies with tests. A research loop verifies with citations. The structural parallel is exact: generate a claim, check it against evidence, revise if wrong, repeat until confidence threshold is met. The difference is that research oracles are softer — a citation either supports a claim or it doesn't, but "supports" lives on a spectrum from "directly states" to "vaguely implies."

This softness is why research loops are harder to build reliably than coding loops. A test either passes or fails — there's no ambiguity. A citation either confirms a claim completely, partially, or not at all — and determining which requires judgment. The oracle for a research loop is therefore a model judge (Level 4 in our hierarchy from Chapter 20), not a deterministic check (Level 1). This makes the loop more expensive per iteration and less certain in its stopping condition.

Despite this, research loops are immensely practical because they automate the most tedious part of knowledge work: tracking down sources, reading them, extracting relevant information, and checking whether the sources actually say what you think they say. A senior engineer doing this manually spends 2-4 hours on a complex research question. A well-built research loop does it in 3-5 minutes for $0.20-$1.00.

This chapter traces a research loop from question to verified answer, using a concrete example throughout: "What are the tradeoffs of SQLite vs PostgreSQL for a 10-user internal tool?"

35.2 The Seven Stages

A research loop has seven distinct stages, each with its own failure modes and verification criteria:


Question Framing → Source Discovery → Information Extraction →
Claim Verification → Confidence Scoring → Synthesis → Stop

Unlike a coding loop (which iterates primarily between edit and verify), a research loop can cycle back to any earlier stage. Finding a contradiction during claim verification may trigger new source discovery. Low confidence scoring may force re-framing the question entirely. The loop is not strictly linear — it's a directed graph with multiple valid paths.


graph TD
    A[Question Framing] --> B[Source Discovery]
    B --> C[Information Extraction]
    C --> D[Claim Verification]
    D --> E[Confidence Scoring]
    E -->|Below threshold| B
    E -->|Contradiction found| A
    E -->|Sufficient| F[Synthesis]
    F --> G[Stop]
    D -->|Citation missing| B
    D -->|Claim contradicted| C

The most common cycle is E→B: confidence is below threshold for a specific sub-question, so the loop searches for more evidence on that topic. The rarest but most expensive cycle is E→A: all evidence points to the wrong question being asked, requiring a full re-frame.

35.3 Stage 1: Question Framing

Vague questions produce vague research. The first stage transforms a user's raw question into a set of precise, answerable sub-questions. This is analogous to the planning phase in a coding loop — breaking a complex task into independently verifiable units.

Input: "What are the tradeoffs of SQLite vs PostgreSQL for a 10-user internal tool?"

This question is answerable but not directly. "Tradeoffs" is multi-dimensional — performance, operations, features, migration, deployment, cost. Without decomposition, the loop would search for everything at once and produce a shallow, unfocused answer.

Framing output:


research_questions = [
    "What are the concurrency limitations of SQLite under 10 simultaneous writers?",
    "What operational overhead does PostgreSQL require vs SQLite (backup, upgrades, monitoring)?",
    "What is the performance difference for read-heavy workloads under 10 users?",
    "What is the performance difference for write-heavy workloads under 10 users?",
    "What deployment complexity does PostgreSQL add vs SQLite (infrastructure, configuration)?",
    "What features does PostgreSQL offer that SQLite lacks, relevant to internal tools?",
    "What is the migration path from SQLite to PostgreSQL if the tool grows beyond 10 users?",
]

Each sub-question is specific enough to have a factual answer backed by documentation or benchmarks. The framing stage also identifies the decision criteria: what would make one answer "better" than another for this user's context (10 users, internal tool)?

Failure mode: Over-scoping. If the framing produces 20+ sub-questions, the research loop will exhaust its budget before reaching synthesis. The depth of research is inversely proportional to the breadth of questions.


MAX_SUB_QUESTIONS = 8

async def frame_question(raw_question: str, context: str) -> list[str]:
    """Transform a vague question into precise, answerable sub-questions."""
    prompt = f"""Given this research question and context, produce {MAX_SUB_QUESTIONS} 
    or fewer precise sub-questions that, when answered, fully address the original.
    
    Question: {raw_question}
    Context: {context}
    
    Requirements:
    - Each sub-question must be answerable with specific evidence (docs, benchmarks, data)
    - Sub-questions should be independent (answering one shouldn't require another)
    - Cover both "pro" and "con" perspectives for each option
    - Prioritize: what does this user's specific context (10 users, internal tool) make most relevant?
    """
    return await model.generate(prompt, response_format=list[str])

35.4 Stage 2: Source Discovery

Source discovery is the equivalent of find_references in a coding loop — locating the raw material that will become evidence. A research loop searches multiple source types, each with different reliability characteristics:

Source Type Authority Currency Bias Risk Best For
Official documentation High High Low (but optimistic) Feature facts, configuration details
Benchmark data High Variable Medium (methodology-dependent) Performance comparisons
Technical blog posts Medium High High (vendor/selection bias) Practitioner experience
Stack Overflow / forums Low-Medium Variable Medium (survivorship bias) Real-world problems encountered
Academic papers High Low (often years old) Low Theoretical foundations
Source code / changelogs Highest Highest None Ground truth about behavior

For our SQLite vs PostgreSQL question, the loop generates search queries that target multiple source types:


search_queries = [
    # Official docs
    "SQLite WAL mode concurrency write locking site:sqlite.org",
    "PostgreSQL MVCC concurrent access site:postgresql.org",
    # Benchmarks
    "SQLite vs PostgreSQL benchmark 2024 2025 small workload",
    # Practitioner experience
    "SQLite production deployment internal tools experience report",
    "PostgreSQL operational overhead small team blog",
    # Migration
    "SQLite to PostgreSQL migration guide lessons learned",
]

Key principle: Cast wide, filter narrow. The discovery phase should return 15-30 sources. The extraction phase will reduce this to 5-10 that actually contain relevant evidence. Starting narrow risks missing important perspectives; you can always discard irrelevant sources, but you can't find evidence you never searched for.

Failure mode: Echo chamber. If all sources are from SQLite advocates (people blogging about why they chose SQLite), the research is biased. A well-designed discovery step explicitly searches for counterarguments:


async def diversify_sources(initial_sources: list[Source]) -> list[Source]:
    """Ensure source diversity by actively seeking opposing viewpoints."""
    # Detect bias in initial results
    sentiments = [classify_sentiment(s, "SQLite", "PostgreSQL") for s in initial_sources]
    
    if all(s.favors == "SQLite" for s in sentiments):
        # Explicitly search for PostgreSQL advantages
        counter_queries = [
            "PostgreSQL advantages over SQLite production",
            "SQLite limitations problems scaling issues",
            "why not SQLite for production applications",
        ]
        counter_sources = await search_all(counter_queries)
        initial_sources.extend(counter_sources)
    
    return initial_sources

35.5 Stage 3: Information Extraction

Each source is fetched, parsed, and distilled into structured claims. This is where the loop transitions from "finding stuff" to "understanding stuff." The model reads each source and extracts specific, verifiable claims relevant to the sub-questions.


from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class ExtractedClaim:
    claim: str                    # The factual assertion
    source_url: str               # Where it came from
    source_title: str             # Human-readable source name
    source_date: str | None       # When the source was published
    confidence: float             # How directly the source states this (0-1)
    quote: str | None             # Direct quote if available
    relevant_to: list[str]        # Which sub-questions this addresses
    
    
# Example extractions from SQLite documentation
claims = [
    ExtractedClaim(
        claim="SQLite supports multiple concurrent readers but only one writer "
              "at a time in WAL mode",
        source_url="https://sqlite.org/wal.html",
        source_title="SQLite WAL Mode Documentation",
        source_date="2024-01",
        confidence=1.0,
        quote="WAL provides more concurrency as readers do not block writers "
              "and a writer does not block readers. Reading and writing can "
              "proceed concurrently.",
        relevant_to=["concurrency_limitations"],
    ),
    ExtractedClaim(
        claim="SQLite write transactions are serialized; concurrent writes "
              "queue behind the active writer with a default 5-second timeout",
        source_url="https://sqlite.org/wal.html",
        source_title="SQLite WAL Mode Documentation",
        source_date="2024-01",
        confidence=0.9,
        quote=None,  # Inferred from documentation about SQLITE_BUSY
        relevant_to=["concurrency_limitations", "write_performance"],
    ),
]

The extraction phase assigns a confidence score to each claim based on how directly the source states it:

  • 1.0: Direct quote from an authoritative source
  • 0.8-0.9: Clearly stated but not quoted verbatim (paraphrased from docs)
  • 0.6-0.7: Implied by the source but not stated directly
  • 0.3-0.5: Inferred from indirect evidence (e.g., a benchmark result implying a design limitation)

Failure mode: Hallucinated extraction. The model "reads" a source and produces a claim that the source doesn't actually make. This happens more often than practitioners expect — the model fills in plausible details from its training data, not from the actual source text. This is why the next stage — claim verification — is essential, not optional.

35.6 Stage 4: Claim Verification

Every extracted claim must be verified against its cited source. This is the research loop's equivalent of running tests. A claim that fails verification is either discarded or sent back for re-sourcing.


from enum import Enum


class VerificationStatus(Enum):
    CONFIRMED = "confirmed"          # Source explicitly states this
    PARTIAL = "partial"              # Source partially supports but claim overstates
    NOT_FOUND = "not_found"          # Source doesn't contain evidence for this
    CONTRADICTED = "contradicted"    # Source says the opposite


async def verify_claim(claim: ExtractedClaim) -> tuple[VerificationStatus, str]:
    """Verify that the source actually supports the claim."""
    # Fetch the source content
    source_content = await fetch_and_parse(claim.source_url)
    
    if source_content is None:
        return VerificationStatus.NOT_FOUND, "Source URL returned 404 or is inaccessible"
    
    # Ask: does this source actually say what we claimed?
    verification = await model.generate(f"""
    Source content (from {claim.source_title}):
    {source_content[:6000]}
    
    Claimed extraction: "{claim.claim}"
    Claimed quote: "{claim.quote or 'None provided'}"
    
    Evaluate whether this source supports the claim:
    - CONFIRMED: Source explicitly states or strongly implies this claim
    - PARTIAL: Source partially supports but the claim overstates or adds unsupported detail
    - NOT_FOUND: Source does not contain evidence for this claim
    - CONTRADICTED: Source provides evidence against this claim
    
    Explain your reasoning in one sentence.
    """)
    
    return parse_verification_result(verification)

Verification catches three classes of errors:

  1. Hallucinated claims: The model generated a plausible-sounding fact that the source doesn't contain. For example, claiming "SQLite supports up to 128 concurrent writers" when the documentation says no such thing.
  1. Overstated claims: The source says "in some workloads, SQLite can match PostgreSQL performance" but the extraction dropped the qualifier to "SQLite matches PostgreSQL performance." The partial support signal catches this.
  1. Outdated claims: The source is from 2019 and describes SQLite behavior before WAL2 mode was proposed. The claim may no longer hold.

Claims that fail verification are either discarded (if the claim was hallucinated) or flagged for re-sourcing (if the claim seems true but the cited source doesn't support it). The loop returns to Stage 2 to find better evidence.

35.7 Stage 5: Confidence Scoring

After verification, the loop assesses whether it has enough verified evidence to answer each sub-question. This is the stopping condition for the inner loop — the equivalent of "all tests pass" in a coding loop.


@dataclass
class SubQuestionConfidence:
    question: str
    verified_claims: list[ExtractedClaim]
    confidence: float  # 0.0 to 1.0
    gaps: list[str]    # What evidence is missing


def score_confidence(
    question: str,
    claims: list[ExtractedClaim],
) -> SubQuestionConfidence:
    """Score how well current evidence answers the sub-question."""
    verified = [c for c in claims if c.verification_status == VerificationStatus.CONFIRMED]
    
    # Confidence heuristics:
    independent_sources = count_independent_sources(verified)
    has_authoritative = any(is_official_docs(c.source_url) for c in verified)
    has_quantitative = any("benchmark" in c.source_title.lower() for c in verified)
    
    if independent_sources >= 3 and has_authoritative:
        base_confidence = 0.95
    elif independent_sources >= 2 and has_authoritative:
        base_confidence = 0.85
    elif independent_sources >= 2:
        base_confidence = 0.75
    elif has_authoritative:
        base_confidence = 0.70
    elif independent_sources >= 1:
        base_confidence = 0.50
    else:
        base_confidence = 0.0
    
    # Penalty for recency: old sources reduce confidence
    avg_age = average_source_age(verified)
    if avg_age > 3:  # More than 3 years old
        base_confidence *= 0.8
    
    gaps = identify_evidence_gaps(question, verified)
    
    return SubQuestionConfidence(
        question=question,
        verified_claims=verified,
        confidence=base_confidence,
        gaps=gaps,
    )

The threshold for "sufficient confidence" depends on the decision stakes:

Threshold Use Case Rationale
0.6 Quick recommendation, user will validate Speed over certainty
0.8 Decision-ready recommendation Default for engineering decisions
0.95 High-stakes, irreversible decision Production migration, architecture choice

If any sub-question falls below threshold, the loop cycles back to source discovery with a targeted query specifically addressing that gap. This targeted re-search is more efficient than broad discovery because the loop now knows exactly what evidence it needs.

35.8 Stage 6: Synthesis

Once all sub-questions meet the confidence threshold, the loop synthesizes findings into a coherent answer. Synthesis is not summarization — it's integration. The loop must resolve tensions between sources, identify the narrative structure that makes the answer most useful, and maintain full citation chains.


async def synthesize(
    original_question: str,
    sub_answers: list[SubQuestionConfidence],
    user_context: str,
) -> str:
    """Combine verified sub-answers into a coherent, actionable response."""
    synthesis_prompt = f"""
    Original question: {original_question}
    User context: {user_context}
    
    Verified findings (with citations):
    {format_findings_with_citations(sub_answers)}
    
    Synthesize into a clear, actionable answer. Requirements:
    - Lead with the recommendation, then support with evidence
    - Acknowledge tradeoffs explicitly — don't pretend one option dominates
    - Cite sources inline for every factual claim using [source_title]
    - Note where evidence is weak or conflicting
    - End with decision guidance: "Choose A when..." vs "Choose B when..."
    - Keep total length under 800 words — density over length
    """
    return await model.generate(synthesis_prompt)

The synthesis stage has its own quality oracle: does the synthesized answer actually use the verified claims? Does every factual statement have a citation? Are there claims in the synthesis that weren't in the verified evidence? This is a final verification pass on the output itself.

35.9 Stage 7: Stopping

The research loop stops when one of three conditions is met:

  1. All sub-questions meet confidence threshold. Primary stop condition — the research is complete.
  2. Budget exhausted. Safety stop — report what's known and explicitly flag what's uncertain.
  3. Diminishing returns. The last N discovery rounds yielded no new verified claims — the available sources have been exhausted.

CONFIDENCE_THRESHOLD = 0.8
MAX_DISCOVERY_ROUNDS = 5
PLATEAU_WINDOW = 2  # Stop if 2 consecutive rounds add no new verified claims

def should_stop(
    sub_questions: list[SubQuestionConfidence],
    discovery_rounds: int,
    claims_added_last_rounds: list[int],  # Claims added in each round
) -> tuple[bool, str]:
    """Determine whether the research loop should terminate."""
    # Primary: all questions answered
    all_confident = all(sq.confidence >= CONFIDENCE_THRESHOLD for sq in sub_questions)
    if all_confident:
        return True, "All sub-questions answered with sufficient confidence."
    
    # Safety: budget exhausted
    if discovery_rounds >= MAX_DISCOVERY_ROUNDS:
        low_conf = [sq for sq in sub_questions if sq.confidence < CONFIDENCE_THRESHOLD]
        return True, f"Budget exhausted. {len(low_conf)} questions below threshold: " + \
               ", ".join(sq.question[:50] for sq in low_conf)
    
    # Plateau: no progress
    if len(claims_added_last_rounds) >= PLATEAU_WINDOW:
        recent = claims_added_last_rounds[-PLATEAU_WINDOW:]
        if all(c == 0 for c in recent):
            return True, "Diminishing returns. No new evidence in last 2 rounds."
    
    return False, ""

35.10 Full Trace: SQLite vs PostgreSQL

Let's trace the complete loop execution for our example.

Round 1: Frame and Discover

The loop frames 7 sub-questions and generates 12 search queries across multiple source types. Discovery returns 22 sources: 4 official documentation pages, 3 benchmark posts, 8 blog posts, 5 Stack Overflow threads, 2 GitHub discussions.

Round 2: Extract and Verify

From 22 sources, the loop extracts 34 claims. Verification results:

  • 28 CONFIRMED (clear support from cited source)
  • 4 PARTIAL (overstated or missing qualifiers)
  • 2 NOT_FOUND (hallucinated details not in the source)

The 2 NOT_FOUND claims are discarded. The 4 PARTIAL claims are rewritten with appropriate qualifiers and re-verified (all pass on second attempt).

Round 3: Score Confidence

Sub-Question Confidence Evidence Quality
Concurrency limitations 0.95 Official docs (sqlite.org) + 2 benchmarks
Operational overhead 0.85 3 independent practitioner reports
Read performance 0.90 2 benchmarks + official docs
Write performance 0.80 1 benchmark + official docs
Deployment complexity 0.75 Only anecdotal blog posts
Feature differences 0.90 Both official docs compared
Migration path 0.60 1 blog post, no authoritative source

Two sub-questions below threshold: deployment complexity (0.75) and migration path (0.60).

Round 4: Targeted Discovery

The loop searches specifically for deployment and migration evidence:

  • "PostgreSQL Docker deployment single developer small team tutorial"
  • "SQLite to PostgreSQL migration SQLAlchemy Django guide 2024 2025"
  • "PostgreSQL vs SQLite deployment complexity comparison infrastructure"

Finds 6 new sources, extracts 8 claims, verifies 7 (1 NOT_FOUND — discarded).

Round 5: Re-Score

All sub-questions now at 0.80+. Deployment complexity reached 0.82 (added a DevOps tutorial as authoritative source). Migration path reached 0.80 (found an SQLAlchemy migration guide with specific steps).

Stop condition met. Proceed to synthesis.

Synthesis output (abbreviated):

For a 10-user internal tool, start with SQLite. It requires zero operational overhead [sqlite.org/serverless.html], deploys as a single file alongside your application, and handles 10 concurrent readers with no performance concern [WAL mode benchmark, 2024].

>

The write serialization limitation is irrelevant at this scale. Write transactions in SQLite complete in 1-5ms for typical CRUD operations [sqlite.org/speed.html]. With 10 users generating writes, queue wait time is negligible — measured in single-digit milliseconds even under sustained load [benchmark-data, 2025].

>

Choose PostgreSQL instead if:
- You need row-level locking for concurrent writes to the same rows (e.g., inventory systems with contention) [postgresql.org/docs/mvcc]
- You need features SQLite lacks: LISTEN/NOTIFY, full-text search beyond FTS5, JSON path queries, materialized views [postgresql.org/docs/features]
- You're certain the tool will grow to 100+ concurrent users within 6 months [practitioner reports suggest this is the threshold where SQLite write contention becomes measurable]

>

Migration path: SQLAlchemy abstracts most differences. Primary friction: datetime handling (SQLite stores as text, PostgreSQL as native type), UPSERT syntax differences, and adding connection pooling. Expect 2-4 hours of migration work for a typical internal tool [SQLAlchemy migration guide, Django migration guide].

Total execution: 5 rounds, 28 source fetches, 39 verified claims, ~$0.35 in model costs, ~3 minutes wall clock.

35.11 Research Loop vs. RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a single-pass approximation of a research loop. RAG retrieves relevant chunks, stuffs them into context, and generates an answer in one forward pass. The research loop iterates. Understanding when to use each is a practical design decision:

Dimension RAG (Single Pass) Research Loop (Multi-Pass)
Source discovery Pre-indexed corpus only Dynamic search across web + corpus
Verification None (trusts retrieved chunks) Explicit claim-by-claim checking
Iteration Zero — one shot 3-7 rounds typical
Confidence tracking Implicit in model uncertainty Explicit numerical scoring
Cost per query $0.01-0.05 $0.20-1.00
Latency 1-3 seconds 1-5 minutes
Reliability Depends entirely on index quality Self-correcting via verification
Handles contradictions Poorly (stuffs both into context) Explicitly (identifies and resolves)

Use RAG when: The corpus is curated and authoritative, the question type is well-understood, answers must be sub-second, and occasional inaccuracies are acceptable (customer-facing search, FAQ bots, internal knowledge base queries).

Use a research loop when: Stakes are high, sources are diverse and possibly contradictory, the answer must be verified and cited, and the user can wait minutes for a thorough answer (engineering decisions, competitive analysis, compliance research, due diligence).

35.12 Failure Modes Unique to Research Loops

Research loops fail in ways that coding loops don't, because their oracles are softer and their domain is unbounded:

Circular sourcing. Multiple sources cite each other, creating false confidence in a claim that originates from a single (possibly wrong) source. Three blog posts all referencing the same Stack Overflow answer aren't three independent confirmations — they're one claim with three copies. Mitigation: trace citations to their origin. If all roads lead to one source, confidence reflects one source, not three.

Temporal decay. A claim verified against a 2022 source may no longer hold in 2026. SQLite's performance characteristics have changed significantly since WAL2 was proposed. PostgreSQL 16 has features that PostgreSQL 12 didn't. Mitigation: weight recent sources higher; flag claims where the newest evidence is older than 2 years; explicitly search for "changes in [topic] since [oldest source year]."

Authority bias. Official documentation describes intended behavior, not necessarily actual behavior. A bug in PostgreSQL's query planner won't appear in the docs. Documentation says "supports concurrent access" but doesn't mention the edge case where concurrent access deadlocks under specific isolation levels. Mitigation: triangulate between docs (what should happen), benchmarks (what measurably happens), and practitioner reports (what actually happens in production).

Scope creep. The loop discovers interesting tangents and chases them, consuming budget without advancing toward the answer. While researching SQLite concurrency, the loop finds an interesting paper on database theory and spends two rounds extracting claims about MVCC that are technically relevant but don't help the user make a decision. Mitigation: every new source discovery must link to a specific sub-question. Claims that don't address any sub-question are extracted but not verified — saving budget for what matters.

Anchoring on first sources. The first sources found shape the loop's understanding, and subsequent sources are interpreted through that lens. If the first three sources are SQLite-positive, the loop may unconsciously discount later PostgreSQL-positive sources. Mitigation: explicitly search for counterarguments after initial discovery, and score confidence on source diversity, not just source count.
