Chapter 11: Make the Claim Before You Touch the Data

“Prove the new agent approval flow makes us safer.” This sentence hides at least six decisions: what “safer” means, which agent actions count, whose experience matters, what alternative policy is being compared, how long outcomes take, and how much evidence is sufficient to change rollout. If analysts open a notebook before resolving those questions, the available data will quietly choose the question for them.

This chapter is a claim-construction workshop. The running case is an engineering agent that proposes shell commands and production changes. The current policy auto-approves low-risk actions and sends others to a human. A new policy requires approval for any command touching deployment infrastructure. We will translate the executive request into a population, unit, intervention, comparison, outcomes, horizon, estimand, evidence grade, and decision rule—then execute an analysis on fixed fixtures.

The Claim Card

A formal claim should fit on one page before implementation begins:

claim_id: AGENT-APPROVAL-07
decision: expand_new_gate | retain_current | revise_and_retest
population: eligible production-agent action requests by staffed teams
unit: action request, clustered within task and team
policy_1: infrastructure-sensitive approval gate v2
policy_0: current gate v1
primary_outcome: harmful execution within 24 hours
guardrails: [task_abandonment, approval_latency, reviewer_minutes]
horizon: 28-day enrollment, 24-hour outcome maturation
estimand: intention-to-treat risk difference
threshold: expand only if upper_95_harm_delta <= -0.002
             and p95_latency_delta <= 45 seconds
exclusions: break-glass incidents, synthetic red-team tasks

The card prevents semantic drift. It does not guarantee identification. It makes disagreements inspectable before results are known.

Question, Claim, Estimand, Decision

A security question is broad: does approval help? A claim is falsifiable: among eligible production action requests, assignment to gate v2 reduces harmful execution within 24 hours by at least 0.2 percentage points without adding more than 45 seconds to p95 decision latency. An estimand is the precise population quantity:

$$\tau_{ITT}=E[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)],$$

where $Y_i(z)$ is the potential harmful-execution outcome under assignment $z$. The decision rule maps estimates and uncertainty to action.

Intention-to-treat is appropriate because assignment is controlled and bypasses occur in reality. A per-protocol estimand—effect among requests that actually received the assigned behavior—answers a different question and can be biased by noncompliance. Both may be reported, but only one is primary.

When random assignment is unavailable, specify the comparison mechanism: phased rollout, regression discontinuity around a policy boundary, difference-in-differences, matched controls, or an explicitly descriptive comparison. Never let “A/B-like” conceal a nonrandom launch.

Choose a Unit That Can Receive Policy

Possible units include event, action request, task, session, identity, host, incident, customer, and team-week. The unit must align with assignment and outcome. One agent task may emit twenty commands. Randomizing commands within the same task can contaminate treatment because an earlier rejection changes later proposals. Meridian therefore randomizes by task and analyzes action outcomes with cluster-aware uncertainty.

The population is not “all logs.” It is the set of units to which the decision will apply. State eligibility in executable terms:

eligible = environment = 'production'
       AND agent_mode = 'write_enabled'
       AND team_staffed_for_review = TRUE
       AND break_glass = FALSE

This excludes unstaffed teams, so external validity is limited. The result cannot justify rollout to them without further evidence. Record eligible-but-not-enrolled units to diagnose selection.

Outcomes Need Adjudication, Not Convenient Proxies

The primary harm outcome is a request that executes and causes unauthorized state change, material policy violation, secret exposure, or service impairment within 24 hours, adjudicated blind to assignment where feasible. “Command rejected” is a control action, not harm. “Security alert” is detector output, not ground truth. “No incident ticket” may reflect reporting failure.

Guardrails protect other objectives. Approval latency should be measured from policy decision request to executable decision, using event time and monotonic duration. Reviewer burden is minutes per eligible task, not minutes per reviewed request alone. Task abandonment needs a definition distinguishing user cancellation from policy-induced failure.

Outcomes form a hierarchy:

  1. harmful execution (primary);
  2. near miss stopped by policy;
  3. unsafe proposal without execution;
  4. review disagreement;
  5. latency, burden, and abandonment.

Moving a result between levels after seeing data is not interpretation; it is outcome switching.

Evidence Grades Describe Support, Not Importance

Meridian grades each input and inference:

GradeMeaningExample
Arandomized or directly verified with complete provenancetask assignment; signed execution trace
Bstrong observational evidence with tested assumptionsphased rollout with stable pretrends
Cindirect or selectively observed evidenceanalyst verdict on alerted actions
Dexpert judgment or unvalidated proxyheuristic “unsafe” tag

A high-impact claim supported by grade D remains high impact and weakly known. Grades never average into a faux confidence score. The claim card lists which conclusion depends on which grade and what evidence would upgrade it.

Evidence can be contradictory. A signed trace may prove a command ran, while incident review disputes whether the change was harmful. Preserve both provenance chains and adjudication. Unknown is a valid state.

Metric Specifications Are Small Programs

A metric specification includes name, purpose, unit, numerator, denominator, event-time rule, availability rule, exclusions, grouping, missingness behavior, revision policy, owner, and tests. For example:

metric: harmful_execution_rate_24h
unit: eligible action request assigned to a policy
numerator: adjudicated harmful execution <= request_time + 24h
denominator: all assigned eligible requests with mature 24h horizon
missing: unresolved outcome remains pending; never coerce to zero
dedupe: request_id, latest valid adjudication version
revision: weekly cohorts may revise for 35 days; retain prior snapshots

The denominator includes assigned requests, not only executed or reviewed requests. That preserves the ITT interpretation. A separate executed-request rate can diagnose mechanism but is post-treatment and must not replace the primary metric.

Assumptions Become Tests or Sensitivity Analyses

Randomization requires allocation integrity, limited interference, known assignment, and valid outcome capture. Test balance on pre-treatment features, check assignment hashes, enumerate cross-task shared state, and compare outcome missingness by arm. Cluster assignment or analysis when teams and tasks create dependence.

For binary outcomes, the risk difference is

$$\widehat\tau=\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0.$$

With rare harm, report arm counts, risk ratio when defined, exact or score intervals, and absolute difference. A zero in one arm does not prove zero risk. For clustered data, use cluster bootstrap, randomization inference, or cluster-robust models. Guardrails often need quantiles, not averages; a modest mean latency can hide a disastrous p95.

Multiplicity matters. One primary outcome and a small preregistered guardrail set protect decision clarity. Exploratory slices are labeled exploratory and used to design follow-up studies, not opportunistically to declare success.

Workshop Artifact: The Approval Evidence Ledger

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Eligible
    Eligible --> AssignedV1: hash bucket
    Eligible --> AssignedV2: hash bucket
    AssignedV1 --> Proposed
    AssignedV2 --> Proposed
    Proposed --> Approved
    Proposed --> Rejected
    Proposed --> Escalated
    Approved --> Executed
    Approved --> Abandoned
    Executed --> Harmful: adjudication
    Executed --> Clean: mature 24h
    Executed --> Pending: horizon open
    Rejected --> NearMiss: counterfactual review

The state machine makes denominators explicit. “Near miss” after rejection is not an observed harmful counterfactual; it is an adjudicated unsafe proposal. The ledger stores assignment before downstream state, immutable request IDs, policy versions, reviewer identity, decision timestamps, execution trace references, outcome version, and evidence grade.

Approval authority itself is a control surface. Reviewers need scoped permissions, conflict-of-interest rules, workload limits, and protected audit logs. The experiment must not weaken production by routing dangerous requests to underprepared reviewers.

Executable Analysis: A Clustered Approval Trial

The fixed fixture contains eight teams, four per policy, with eligible request counts, harmful executions, p95 latency, and reviewer minutes. The analysis reports ITT harm rates and a team-cluster bootstrap confidence interval. Save as ch11_approval_analysis.py and run with the specified .venv.

import numpy as np

rows = [
    ("A", 0, 400, 5, 31, 180), ("B", 0, 350, 4, 29, 155),
    ("C", 0, 500, 7, 35, 225), ("D", 0, 450, 5, 33, 205),
    ("E", 1, 420, 2, 63, 300), ("F", 1, 380, 1, 68, 285),
    ("G", 1, 510, 3, 72, 390), ("H", 1, 440, 2, 65, 325),
]

def rate(group):
    selected = [r for r in rows if r[1] == group]
    return sum(r[3] for r in selected) / sum(r[2] for r in selected)

r0, r1 = rate(0), rate(1)
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
by_arm = [[r for r in rows if r[1] == z] for z in (0, 1)]
deltas = []
for _ in range(20_000):
    sampled = []
    for arm in by_arm:
        sampled.append([arm[i] for i in rng.integers(0, len(arm), len(arm))])
    arm_rates = [sum(r[3] for r in a) / sum(r[2] for r in a) for a in sampled]
    deltas.append(arm_rates[1] - arm_rates[0])
lo, hi = np.quantile(deltas, [0.025, 0.975])
lat0 = max(r[4] for r in by_arm[0]); lat1 = max(r[4] for r in by_arm[1])
minutes = [sum(r[5] for r in arm) / sum(r[2] for r in arm) for arm in by_arm]

print(f"v1_harm={r0:.4%} v2_harm={r1:.4%}")
print(f"risk_difference={r1-r0:.4%} cluster_bootstrap_95=[{lo:.4%}, {hi:.4%}]")
print(f"max_team_p95_latency_delta={lat1-lat0}s")
print(f"review_minutes_per_request: v1={minutes[0]:.3f} v2={minutes[1]:.3f}")
print("decision=REVISE_AND_RETEST")

Exact fixture output:

v1_harm=1.2353% v2_harm=0.4571%
risk_difference=-0.7782% cluster_bootstrap_95=[-0.9484%, -0.6231%]
max_team_p95_latency_delta=37s
review_minutes_per_request: v1=0.450 v2=0.743
decision=REVISE_AND_RETEST

Why not expand? The harm criterion and latency guardrail pass, but reviewer burden rises 71 percent and the claim card did not define an acceptable burden threshold. The correct action is not to invent one after seeing results. Operations and security must set it, improve routing, then retest. The fixture teaches a crucial discipline: favorable statistics do not authorize a decision absent a complete decision contract.

The bootstrap resamples teams, reflecting assignment clusters. Eight clusters are too few for comfortable asymptotics; a real launch should use randomization inference and more clusters. The exact output is a software fixture, not a claim that the interval has ideal frequentist coverage.

Decision Logs Prevent Retrospective Certainty

Before launch, record the claim, owner, alternatives, approved evidence, analysis version, thresholds, stop conditions, and expiration. After analysis, append results and decision without overwriting the prior record. If leadership chooses rollout despite an unmet condition, record it as a risk acceptance with accountable authority.

A good log distinguishes “evidence says,” “model assumes,” and “decision chooses.” Analysts estimate; policy owners weigh costs; control owners implement; independent reviewers challenge. Combining these roles invites motivated reasoning.

Negative Controls and Falsification

A credible plan says what should not change. The approval gate should not affect read-only actions outside its scope. If their latency shifts, shared infrastructure or logging may explain the apparent effect. A pre-treatment outcome—harm recorded before assignment—must show no policy effect; a nonzero estimate reveals linkage or clock defects.

Falsification questions make the workshop adversarial. Could reviewers infer assignment and classify outcomes differently? Could the gate cause agents to split one dangerous command into several safe-looking commands? Could users move work to uninstrumented tools? Could latency shrink the denominator through abandonment? Each mechanism implies an auxiliary measure and sometimes a redesigned unit.

The estimand may be partially identified. If capture is missing for two percent of v1 and six percent of v2 requests, calculate worst-case bounds by assigning all missing v2 outcomes harmful and missing v1 outcomes benign, then reverse. If the decision changes across bounds, repair capture before rollout.

Power calculations should use the decision threshold and cluster structure, not a generic ritual. Estimate baseline harm, minimum worthwhile absolute reduction, intracluster correlation, cluster sizes, and attrition. For extremely rare harm, extend enrollment, use a validated intermediate outcome, apply controlled sequential monitoring, or combine capability tests with bounded observational evidence. Do not lower the evidentiary bar because the desired answer is expensive.

Claims also need transport conditions. A result from staffed infrastructure teams may transfer to another group only if action mix, reviewer skill, baseline risk, tool permissions, and outcome capture are sufficiently comparable. List those moderators before rollout. Re-estimate locally when they differ rather than applying the same threshold by organizational decree.

Archive abandoned claims as carefully as successful ones. A stopped study records why identification failed, which data were insufficient, and what future instrumentation would make the question answerable. This prevents another team from repeating the same attractive but invalid analysis and turns negative analytical work into institutional evidence.

Interview Practicum

The interviewer says: “Prove our new agent approval gate makes us safer.” The candidate must ask questions; each good question unlocks a fact. Assignment is by team, not request. Teams share reviewers. Harm labels mature after 24 hours. The rollout excludes nights because nobody staffs approvals. Leadership wants results after one week. The new gate logs rejected commands but the old gate does not.

The strong response converts the request into an ITT claim for staffed eligible teams, names task or team as assignment unit, defines harmful execution and guardrails, and refuses to compare raw rejection counts because instrumentation differs. They flag one week as inadequate for stable team-level inference and external validity to nights.

Then the interviewer changes the constraint: randomization is politically impossible. The candidate proposes a phased rollout with pretrend checks, contemporaneous controls, explicit interference analysis, and a weaker evidence grade. When told the highest-risk team demands early treatment, they recognize treatment timing is confounded and avoid claiming a simple causal effect.

Worked Answer

Draft and approve the claim card before rollout. Instrument eligibility, assignment, proposal, review, execution, outcome, and availability times with immutable IDs. Randomize at the task or team level depending shared context. Blind outcome adjudication where feasible. Retain all assigned eligible units in the primary ITT analysis.

Estimate absolute harm-rate difference with cluster-aware uncertainty. Report counts and unresolved outcomes. Pre-register p95 latency, reviewer burden, abandonment, and bypass as guardrails with thresholds. Audit allocation integrity and differential missingness. Publish evidence grades and limitations. Expand only when the complete decision rule passes; otherwise revise, retest, or explicitly accept risk.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

What if no harmful events occur? Report the exposure count and an upper confidence bound. Zero observations is not zero risk; supplement with safe adversarial tests that answer capability, not prevalence.

Can rejected unsafe commands count as prevented harm? They are near misses or unsafe proposals. Counterfactual harm requires assumptions or a design; do not count every rejection as a prevented incident.

Why not use requests as independent observations? Shared teams, tasks, reviewers, and repositories induce correlation. Ignoring clustering understates uncertainty.

Who sets the threshold? Accountable security and product owners set it before results, informed by loss, friction, and capacity. Analysts make consequences and uncertainty explicit.

Key Takeaways

  • Formalize population, unit, policy contrast, outcomes, horizon, estimand, and decision before analysis.
  • Assignment unit and analysis unit need not match; dependence must be honored.
  • Control actions and detector outputs are not security outcomes.
  • Evidence grades describe inferential support and must remain traceable to claims.
  • Metric specifications define denominator, time, missingness, revisions, ownership, and tests.
  • A favorable estimate cannot approve rollout when the decision contract is incomplete.
