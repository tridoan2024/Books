Chapter 46: Case Study: Identity Compromise Detection

Identity compromise is not a row-classification problem. An account can be legitimate at 09:00, have its refresh token stolen at 09:12, and be used simultaneously by its owner and attacker at 09:20. The evidence is distributed across authentication, device, session, privilege, application, and recovery systems. The intervention changes what becomes observable: a challenge stops some attacks, annoys some users, and selectively produces labels. A useful system must therefore model time, relationships, uncertainty, action capacity, and causal friction together.

This capstone designs Helios, an enterprise compromise system for workforce identities. Its unit of decision is not “the user” but an identity episode: a bounded interval in which related credentials, sessions, devices, resources, and actions may share control. Helios may permit, silently monitor, require step-up authentication, revoke a session, or route an episode to an investigator. It does not automatically disable an employee from one anomalous event.

Temporal identity graph

The graph has typed nodes and interval-valued edges:

  • Person, Account, Credential, Session, Device, Network, Application, Resource, and Case nodes;
  • OWNS, AUTHENTICATED_WITH, ISSUED, OBSERVED_ON, ACCESSED, ASSUMED_ROLE, and REVIEWED_AS edges;
  • valid_from, valid_to, observed_at, source, and confidence on every edge.

Event time and ingestion time are distinct. A mobile device can upload an authentication event late; a directory correction can change the historical owner mapping. Point-in-time features use only graph state known at decision time. Otherwise the model “learns” from a later analyst verdict or asset enrichment.

flowchart LR
 A[Auth/device/app events] --> N[Normalize and resolve entities]
 N --> G[(Bitemporal identity graph)]
 G --> E[Episode builder]
 E --> F[Point-in-time feature service]
 F --> B[Rules and behavioral baselines]
 F --> M[Calibrated supervised model]
 B --> C[Risk composer]
 M --> C
 C --> P[Capacity-aware intervention policy]
 P --> X[Allow / step-up / revoke / investigate]
 X --> O[Outcome and friction ledger]
 O --> G
 O --> Q[Evaluation and causal analysis]

An episode begins with a credential issuance or authentication and links later events while continuity evidence remains plausible. It closes after inactivity, explicit revocation, or a maximum horizon. Concurrent sessions remain separate but share account and credential ancestors. This permits a “good owner plus bad token” representation rather than forcing one label onto the account.

The missingness mask is a feature and a health signal. “No device evidence” differs from “known unmanaged device.” Entity-resolution confidence propagates into risk rather than disappearing after a deterministic join.

Labels that admit uncertainty

Confirmed compromise is rare and delayed. Helios combines sources without pretending they are equivalent:

EvidenceLabel treatment
user reports “not me,” forensic token theft, attacker infrastructure matchhigh-confidence positive
analyst-confirmed benign travel or approved automationhigh-confidence negative for that episode
successful phishing-resistant step-upweak negative; attacker may have abandoned
password reset, help-desk recovery, bulk session revokeweak positive
no case openedunlabeled, not negative

Let latent compromise be \(Y\), labeling functions be \(L_j\in\{-1,0,1\}\), and their conditional accuracies be learned from overlap and a small adjudicated set. A generative weak-label model estimates

\[ q_i=P(Y_i=1\mid L_{i1},\ldots,L_{im}), \]

while accounting for correlated functions such as “password reset” and “help-desk recovery.” These soft targets can train a discriminative model, but evaluation uses time-separated, independently reviewed episodes. Cases sampled only above the old threshold are corrected with randomized below-threshold audits and inverse-probability weighting.

Reviewers see evidence available at decision time plus separately marked later evidence. They assign compromised, benign, indeterminate, or mixed-control, record confidence, and identify the controlled credential or session. Double review is concentrated where disagreement changes policy, not spread uniformly.

Three model layers and calibration

Helios deliberately keeps three layers. Deterministic rules catch high-specificity facts such as an impossible refresh-token binding or use after revocation. Behavioral baselines compare an episode with the identity's own history and an appropriate peer group. A supervised gradient model combines stable graph and sequence features. The composer records which layer contributed; a model outage therefore degrades to rules instead of failing open.

A useful sequence statistic is the decayed surprise of event \(e_t\):

\[ S_t=\sum_{k<t}\exp[-(t-t_k)/\tau],[-\log P(e_t\mid context_{t_k})]. \]

Graph fanout and privilege escalation then distinguish an unusual login from an episode that rapidly touches many sensitive resources.

Raw scores are calibrated on a later time block using isotonic regression or Platt scaling, stratified when major populations have different base rates. Reliability diagrams, Brier score, and expected calibration error accompany precision-recall results. Calibration is monitored by model release, authentication method, region, workforce type, and sensor availability. An apparently calibrated aggregate can hide severe contractor overestimation and administrator underestimation.

From risk to intervention

An intervention is selected by expected loss, capacity, and constraints:

\[ a^*(x)=\arg\min_a\{p(x)L_{miss}(a)+(1-p(x))L_{friction}(a)+L_{ops}(a)\}. \]

Actions have different attack interruption and user costs. Silent monitoring is cheap but weak. A phishing-resistant step-up is stronger and measurable. Session revocation may destroy unsaved work. Account disablement can block emergency operations and requires corroboration.

The policy is monotone in calibrated risk but context-sensitive. At equal risk, a privileged admin accessing a production control plane may receive a stronger action than an employee reading a public wiki. Capacity is explicit: if investigators can handle 60 episodes per day, ranking is by expected avoidable loss, not raw anomaly. Helios abstains when key sensors are missing or entity resolution is ambiguous and routes the episode to a safer action.

Guardrails prohibit use of protected personal attributes, cap repeated challenges, provide accessible recovery, and prevent an attacker from weaponizing the detector to lock out a victim. Explanation codes state evidence such as new_hardware_key + token_reuse_after_revocation; they do not expose a recipe precise enough to aid evasion.

Safe SQL lab: build temporal episodes

The following SQLite lab creates a tiny event stream and calculates episode-level signals. It uses synthetic data and no credentials.

CREATE TABLE events(
  event_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  account TEXT, session TEXT, event_time TEXT,
  event_type TEXT, device TEXT, country TEXT,
  privilege_delta INTEGER, resource TEXT
);

INSERT INTO events VALUES
(1,'alice','s1','2026-04-03 09:00:00','login','laptop-A','US',0,'mail'),
(2,'alice','s1','2026-04-03 09:04:00','access','laptop-A','US',0,'docs'),
(3,'alice','s2','2026-04-03 09:07:00','login','unknown-X','NL',0,'mail'),
(4,'alice','s2','2026-04-03 09:08:00','assume_role','unknown-X','NL',1,'cloud-admin'),
(5,'alice','s2','2026-04-03 09:09:00','access','unknown-X','NL',0,'payroll'),
(6,'bob','s3','2026-04-03 10:00:00','login','laptop-B','US',0,'mail');

WITH session_rollup AS (
 SELECT account, session, MIN(event_time) AS start_time,
        COUNT(DISTINCT resource) AS resources,
        MAX(privilege_delta) AS privilege_jump,
        COUNT(DISTINCT device) AS devices,
        MIN(country) AS country
 FROM events GROUP BY account, session
), overlap AS (
 SELECT a.account, a.session,
        EXISTS(SELECT 1 FROM session_rollup b
          WHERE b.account=a.account AND b.session<>a.session
          AND ABS(strftime('%s',b.start_time)-strftime('%s',a.start_time)) < 900)
          AS concurrent_session
 FROM session_rollup a
)
SELECT s.account, s.session, s.resources, s.privilege_jump,
       o.concurrent_session,
       20*s.privilege_jump + 15*o.concurrent_session + 5*(s.resources-1) AS risk_points
FROM session_rollup s JOIN overlap o USING(account,session)
ORDER BY risk_points DESC, account, session;

Expected output:

alice|s2|3|1|1|45
alice|s1|2|0|1|20
bob|s3|1|0|0|0

This is an explainable feature calculation, not a production detector. It reveals an important modeling issue: Alice's legitimate session receives overlap points because the graph contains a suspicious sibling. A production composer should allocate evidence to the likely controlled session while retaining a shared episode link. Run the lab with sqlite3, or through Python's standard-library sqlite3 module when the CLI is unavailable.

Causal evaluation of step-up friction

Observed challenge success is not the causal effect of step-up. High-risk episodes receive the treatment, so naive treated-versus-untreated comparisons are confounded. Define two primary estimands for eligible episodes:

\[ ATE_{attack}=E[Y(1)-Y(0)], \qquad ATE_{friction}=E[F(1)-F(0)], \]

where \(Y\) is compromise completion and \(F\) is user time lost. Because withholding a control from extreme-risk cases is unethical, randomize only within an equipoise band between two acceptable policies—for example immediate phishing-resistant step-up versus step-up at the first sensitive action. Always-treat high-risk cases and never-treat low-risk cases remain observational.

Instrument assignment before outcome, analyze intent-to-treat, and use cluster-robust errors when a user can contribute repeated episodes. Outcomes include confirmed downstream compromise, session revocation, sensitive action completion, challenge success, recovery invocation, abandonment, time to resume work, repeated challenges, and support contacts. Estimate heterogeneous effects for admins, mobile users, accessibility cohorts, and device health, with privacy review.

When randomization is impossible, emulate a target trial using the policy threshold. Regression discontinuity near a stable calibrated-risk cutoff can estimate a local effect if actors cannot precisely manipulate the score and no other action changes at the cutoff. Interrupted policies, detector changes, and label delay must be modeled. Report sensitivity to unmeasured confounding rather than calling a propensity-weighted association causal by default.

Operations and failure drills

Offline replay without temporal leakage

Identity data makes leakage unusually easy. “Device later marked compromised,” a case opened after the decision, or a corrected owner mapping can yield spectacular offline results while being unavailable online. Helios materializes every row with an as_of_time and joins only records whose knowledge time precedes it. Labels have a separate available_after timestamp. Feature code runs identically in backfill and streaming, and replay compares the online decision payload with later reconstruction.

Splits are forward in time and grouped by account, credential family, or campaign where appropriate. A random row split would place adjacent events from one takeover in train and test. Evaluation includes cold-start users, new devices, and degraded sensors. Visible baselines—a few high-specificity rules, per-identity frequencies, and calibrated logistic regression—show whether graph complexity earns its cost.

Fairness, privacy, and recovery

Geography, schedule, disability accommodations, travel, and employment type can proxy for sensitive traits. Review asks whether a feature is necessary for an attack mechanism and whether a less invasive signal exists. Location precision is reduced when network class suffices; peer groups have minimum sizes; trace access is purpose-limited and audited.

Fairness is not equal challenge rates despite different exposure. Compare calibration, false interventions, recovery time, repeated challenge burden, and attack misses, then change features or policy where differences lack security justification. Recovery is part of control efficacy. If a legitimate traveler cannot recover, the system created an availability incident; if recovery uses weak questions, it becomes an attacker bypass. Recovery events return to the graph as security and friction outcomes.

Graph split drill. A device-ID migration fragments one device into thousands of nodes, causing novelty and challenge rates to spike. Freeze model promotion, compare source cardinality and resolution confidence, replay with corrected mappings, and reimburse users through automatic challenge suppression where corroborating signals are absent.

Adversarial low-and-slow drill. An attacker uses a familiar residential proxy and waits between actions. Test whether credential binding, resource sequence, graph fanout, and post-auth privilege evidence compensate for lost geolocation surprise. Do not simply lower thresholds globally.

Selective-label drill. Analysts only investigate top-ranked episodes for a month. Measure label propensity by score band, launch a safe random audit below threshold, reweight evaluation, and show how the apparent precision was inflated.

Step-up outage drill. The authentication provider fails. High-risk sensitive actions fail closed under a pre-agreed continuity policy; ordinary low-risk sessions degrade to monitoring with short expirations. The incident report distinguishes security exceptions from authentication availability.

Operational SLOs include point-in-time feature freshness, graph resolution latency, decision p95, challenge availability, intervention reversal time, daily review capacity, calibration drift, and outcome-link completeness. On-call dashboards show rates and denominators: a fall in detections can mean fewer attacks, missing events, or a broken join.

Sources and Further Reading

  • NIST, Digital Identity Guidelines SP 800-63B, grounds authenticator assurance, replay resistance, and recovery; risk scoring does not replace strong authentication.
  • NIST, Zero Trust Architecture SP 800-207, supports continuous, resource-aware decisions rather than permanent trust after login.
  • MITRE ATT&CK, Valid Accounts, explains why legitimate credentials make post-authentication sequence and resource evidence essential.
  • Guo et al., On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks, motivates post-hoc calibration, but temporal and population drift require continuing validation.
  • Ratner et al., Snorkel, provides a foundation for estimating latent labels from noisy labeling functions; correlated operational labels must be modeled explicitly.
  • Hernán and Robins, Causal Inference: What If, informs target-trial specification and the separation of prediction from intervention effects.

Interview Practicum

A global company challenges the top 0.5% of workforce sessions. Confirmed takeovers fell 25%, but help-desk calls rose 40%. Administrators complain of repeated prompts. Labels exist almost exclusively for challenged sessions, and a device-telemetry migration occurred halfway through the quarter. The CISO asks whether to expand the system to customers. Design the decision analysis.

Worked Answer

I would refuse the before/after causal claim. The policy selected the labels, the device migration changed features and missingness, and attacker pressure may have changed. First I would reconstruct bitemporal episodes under both identity-resolution versions and quantify event coverage, graph fragmentation, score shifts, challenge propensity, and outcome linkage by week and population.

For predictive quality I would create an independently adjudicated, time-forward sample containing challenged and unchallenged episodes. Random audits below the threshold identify missed compromise and permit inverse-propensity correction. I would evaluate calibration and expected avoidable loss, not accuracy, with separate administrator and device-health strata.

For intervention effect I would define eligible episodes and two outcomes: downstream harmful action and total user time lost, including recovery. Extreme-risk sessions stay protected. In an equipoise band I would randomize between two acceptable timings or challenge mechanisms, analyze intent-to-treat, cap repeat prompts, and monitor compromise, abandonment, help-desk demand, and accessibility. If randomization is unavailable, I would consider a threshold design only after proving the score and other controls were stable around the cutoff.

I would not transfer workforce thresholds to customers. Customer base rates, devices, recovery channels, loss functions, and tolerance differ. I would launch customer shadow scoring, build customer-specific calibration and friction baselines, complete abuse and lockout threat modeling, and test recovery capacity. The executive recommendation would separate: evidence the detector ranks risk; evidence step-up prevents harm; and evidence the customer workflow has acceptable net benefit.

At Principal scope I would also change the operating system: versioned entity resolution, randomized label audits as a permanent budget item, policy-independent outcome collection, a challenge-frequency constraint, and an accountable owner for each population's calibration. That prevents the next quarter from reproducing the same uncertainty.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

What if the CISO needs one threshold tomorrow? Choose a conservative threshold from calibrated expected loss and capacity, retain hard rules for incontrovertible signals, cap repeated challenges, and label it an interim policy with a dated reevaluation.

Why not train on successful challenges as benign? Success proves possession of the requested factor, not that the preceding session was benign; an attacker may abandon or a compromised factor may satisfy the challenge.

How do you handle mixed control? Label the session or credential, not the whole account. Preserve concurrent legitimate and malicious branches in the graph.

Which metric belongs on the executive dashboard? Estimated harmful sessions prevented with an uncertainty interval, paired with hours of user friction and recovery burden, plus telemetry and calibration health.

Key Takeaways

  • Model compromise as temporal control of sessions and credentials, not a permanent user label.
  • Preserve event time, knowledge time, entity confidence, and missingness in the graph.
  • Weak operational signals are useful only when their selection and dependence are explicit.
  • Calibrated risk should drive a capacity-aware intervention policy, not an anomaly leaderboard.
  • Evaluate prevention and friction as causal outcomes with ethical experimentation or carefully bounded quasi-experiments.
