Chapter 44: Communicating Evidence for Decisions

The most consequential security memo is often written when the evidence does not resolve the question. A control evaluation may show fewer incidents but have wide intervals, changed telemetry, low adoption, and plausible attacker displacement. “No statistically significant effect” does not mean “no effect.” A confident recommendation can exceed the evidence; an evasive summary can leave leaders unaware that delay is itself a choice.

This chapter builds a decision-memo system for such cases. The running example is a step-up authentication control evaluated over eight weeks. The estimate suggests benefit, but the study is underpowered for rare account takeover and clearly shows user friction. The system must preserve facts, estimates, assumptions, forecasts, and recommendations as different objects; visualize uncertainty without hiding consequences; track whether forecasters are calibrated; and stage authority as evidence improves.

The Memo Is a Versioned Decision Interface

A durable memo is not a polished narrative detached from evidence. It is a compact interface between analysis and accountable choice. Use this structure:

  1. Decision and deadline. Who decides what, by when, and what happens if no choice is made?
  2. Recommendation stage. Observe, instrument, pilot, expand, hold, restrict, roll back, or retire.
  3. One-sentence rationale. State the benefit signal, uncertainty, and principal cost.
  4. Options. Include status quo and at least one reversible alternative.
  5. Evidence ledger. Separate observed facts, estimates, assumptions, and external evidence.
  6. Uncertainty and sensitivity. Show intervals, denominators, missingness, and which assumptions change the choice.
  7. Forecasts. Record probabilities for decision-relevant future events with resolution criteria.
  8. Guardrails and ownership. Define stop conditions, monitoring, and accountable owners.
  9. Next evidence. Name the experiment or instrumentation that has decision value.
  10. Decision record. Capture choice, approver, date, dissent, and review trigger.
stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Question
    Question --> EvidenceLedger: define decision and alternatives
    EvidenceLedger --> Forecast: facts / estimates / assumptions
    Forecast --> StageChoice: calibrated probabilities + sensitivity
    StageChoice --> Pilot: reversible evidence supports learning
    StageChoice --> Hold: downside or uncertainty too high
    StageChoice --> Expand: benefit robust and guardrails pass
    Pilot --> Expand: mature outcomes pass gates
    Pilot --> Restrict: guardrail breach
    Pilot --> Hold: value of more evidence exceeds rollout value
    Expand --> Review: scheduled or trigger-based
    Hold --> EvidenceLedger: new evidence
    Restrict --> EvidenceLedger: incident analysis
    Review --> [*]: retire or renew decision

The diagram makes “pilot” a decision state, not a weak compromise. A staged recommendation specifies authority, population, duration, evidence to collect, and precommitted transitions.

Evidence Ledger: Stop Mixing Epistemic Categories

Consider the sentence: “The control reduced account takeover by 30% and should be launched globally.” It combines an estimate, a causal claim, and a recommendation. Replace it with a ledger:

Facts can still be wrong, estimates depend on methods, assumptions can be challenged, and recommendations depend on values. The categories do not assign prestige; they expose what kind of disagreement is occurring.

Negative evidence belongs in the ledger. Record instrumentation gaps, outcome definitions that changed, tests that failed to replicate, and segments with insufficient sample. An executive should not have to infer absences from a footnote.

Inconclusive Does Not Mean Neutral

Let \(\Delta\) be change in confirmed takeover rate, where negative is beneficial. Suppose the 95% interval is [-1.8, +0.6] incidents per 10,000 accounts. The estimate does not establish benefit at a conventional threshold, but it also does not establish equivalence. Ask decision questions:

  • How much probability lies below the minimum worthwhile benefit?
  • How much lies above the maximum tolerable harm?
  • What are costs and reversibility of each option?
  • What evidence would arrive by waiting?
  • What harm or learning is lost during delay?

A frequentist confidence interval is not a probability distribution over the effect. If decision makers need probabilities such as P(Delta < -0.5), use a declared Bayesian model or bootstrap decision simulation with its assumptions, not an informal reinterpretation of a confidence interval.

For action \(a\) and uncertain state \(\theta\), compare expected loss:

\[ EL(a)=\int L(a,\theta)p(\theta\mid D)d\theta+C(a). \]

Then run sensitivity over disputed losses and priors. If the recommendation changes whenever the cost of friction moves slightly, say the decision is preference-sensitive. If every plausible setting favors a limited pilot, the staged recommendation is robust even when the global-effect claim is not.

Visualizing Uncertainty for Rare Security Outcomes

Do not use a red/amber/green cell to summarize an interval that crosses important thresholds. A useful effect plot includes:

  • a zero-effect line;
  • a minimum worthwhile benefit boundary;
  • a maximum tolerable harm boundary;
  • point and interval estimates for the overall population and predeclared segments;
  • raw event counts and denominators;
  • a note on assignment unit and outcome maturity.

For the example, a textual version is:

more benefit <---------------- 0 ----------------> more harm
        worthwhile -0.5                 intolerable +0.2
overall        [---------●--------------------]   -1.8 to +0.6
managed tier      [-----●----------]             -1.5 to +0.1
consumer       [------------●-----------------]   -2.0 to +0.9

This is not a forest plot substitute in publication; it shows the semantics a plot must carry. Avoid truncating the axis at the point estimate, mapping probability to color without a legend, or hiding a zero-event numerator. “0 of 2,000” is bounded evidence, not zero risk. If no failures are observed in \(n\) approximately independent trials, the rough 95% upper bound 3/n is a useful orientation. If observations cluster by repository, user, session, or model version, show the cluster count and use an effective-sample-size or cluster-bootstrap analysis; the raw event count can materially overstate certainty.

Pair the effect plot with an outcome table. Executives need consequences: expected takeovers avoided, additional challenges, support tickets, analyst workload, latency, and affected customer groups under low/base/high scenarios.

Forecasts Make Beliefs Auditable

Decision memos often contain implicit predictions: “the interval will narrow,” “attackers will not displace,” or “support volume will normalize.” Convert them into resolvable forecasts.

A forecast record contains question, probability, opening evidence, forecaster, timestamp, close date, resolution source, and ambiguity rule. Example: “By 2026-11-30, the six-week pilot will show at least 0.5 fewer confirmed takeovers per 10,000 eligible accounts without challenge abandonment exceeding 0.6 percentage points.” This compound question may be split into two forecasts so resolution is diagnostic.

For binary outcome \(y\) and forecast \(p\), Brier score is (p-y)^2. Across forecasts, calibration asks whether events assigned 70% occur about 70% of the time. Sharpness asks whether forecasts move away from 50%; sharp but miscalibrated confidence is not skill.

Use calibration bins only with enough observations and show counts. Also report mean Brier score against simple baselines, such as historical frequency or always 50%. Security forecasts are correlated and selectively created, so do not overinterpret a small leaderboard. The goal is learning: which teams, horizons, and event types are overconfident, and whether memo probabilities improve after feedback.

Never score recommendations as if they were forecasts. “Run a pilot” is a choice. Score its embedded empirical predictions, such as workload, effect, or guardrail breach.

Safe Lab: Score and Calibrate a Forecast Register

This standard-library lab evaluates ten resolved forecasts. It computes Brier score and reliability by probability bucket. The cases are synthetic.

forecasts = [
 (0.9,1),(0.8,1),(0.8,0),(0.7,1),(0.7,1),
 (0.6,0),(0.4,0),(0.3,1),(0.2,0),(0.1,0),
]
brier=sum((p-y)**2 for p,y in forecasts)/len(forecasts)
buckets={"low":[],"mid":[],"high":[]}
for p,y in forecasts:
    key="low" if p<0.34 else "high" if p>=0.67 else "mid"
    buckets[key].append((p,y))
print("brier",round(brier,3))
for key in ("low","mid","high"):
    rows=buckets[key]
    mean_p=sum(p for p,_ in rows)/len(rows)
    observed=sum(y for _,y in rows)/len(rows)
    print(key,"n",len(rows),"forecast",round(mean_p,2),"observed",round(observed,2))
assert round(brier,3)==0.193

Expected output:

brier 0.193
low n 3 forecast 0.2 observed 0.33
mid n 2 forecast 0.5 observed 0.0
high n 5 forecast 0.78 observed 0.8

The high bucket is close to calibrated in this tiny sample, while the middle bucket is not. With only two cases, that is a prompt for inspection, not a stable conclusion. Extend the lab by comparing the register with a 50% baseline (Brier = 0.25) and by separating 30-day operational forecasts from one-year threat forecasts.

Staged Recommendations

A recommendation should bind action to evidence strength and reversibility.

retireremove controlno decision value, excessive cost, or unsupported claimarchive evidence and migration plan

For the step-up control, a defensible recommendation might be: “Do not launch globally. Expand from 5% to 15% in managed enterprise tenants for six weeks; exclude accessibility-sensitive flows; stop if abandonment exceeds +0.6 pp for three days or any severe account-lockout incident is confirmed; expand further only if the mature posterior probability of at least 0.5 fewer takeovers per 10,000 exceeds 80% and support capacity remains below 70%.”

The exact Bayesian gate is a governance choice requiring a documented model and prior. The strength is not the number 80%; it is the precommitted transition, consequence bound, and review date.

Production Failure Drill: The Slide That Lost Its Interval

An analyst’s effect plot shows a 30% reduction with a wide interval. During executive editing, the interval and denominator disappear, leaving a large green arrow. The CISO approves global rollout. This is an analytical integrity incident even though no database failed.

Run a tabletop with versioned memo artifacts. Detection may come from the analyst, an approver, or an automated check requiring uncertainty fields. Containment pauses authority expansion and sends a corrected decision brief. Preserve the analysis, original memo, edited slide, approvals, and distribution list. Determine which decisions relied on the altered representation.

Recovery requires re-presenting options with interval, counts, friction, and staged alternative; the accountable executive then reaffirms or changes the decision. The post-incident control is not a ban on slides. Require decision-critical charts to carry an evidence ID and generated caption; material edits after analytical sign-off trigger reapproval; dissent and limitations remain attached to the decision record.

The analyst owns accuracy of the estimate, the memo owner owns faithful translation, and the decision maker owns the chosen action. Communications owns distribution but does not own the evidence claim.

Tailoring Without Changing the Evidence

Engineers need schema, test, and failure details. Operators need workload, thresholds, runbooks, and failure signals. Product leaders need adoption, user burden, and options. Executives need the decision, consequence range, reversibility, and owner. These are views over one ledger, not separate truths.

A 30-second summary for the case is: “The pilot points toward fewer takeovers but is too small to establish the size of benefit; it clearly adds 0.42 percentage points of abandonment. I recommend a six-week expansion limited to managed tenants, with explicit abandonment and lockout stops, before any global launch.”

The technical appendix then documents assignment, missingness, effect model, sensitivity, segment results, and code manifest. Concision at the top is safe only because traceability exists below.

Sources and Further Reading

  • NIST, Visualizing Uncertainty provides research context for presenting uncertainty rather than suppressing it.
  • NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods supplies statistical foundations for intervals, experimental analysis, and graphical methods.
  • NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 emphasizes governance and communicating cybersecurity risk to decision makers.
  • Tetlock and Gardner, Superforecasting motivates resolvable questions, probability estimates, and feedback; organizational use still requires care about incentives and selection.
  • Gneiting and Raftery, Strictly Proper Scoring Rules supplies the formal basis for evaluating probabilistic forecasts without rewarding dishonest probability reports.
  • Gelman et al., Bayesian Workflow supports iterative model criticism and sensitivity rather than treating posterior output as automatic truth.

The synthesis is that uncertainty communication becomes operational when it is versioned, resolvable, and tied to staged authority. A chart informs; a decision system records how evidence changes action and later checks whether its forecasts deserved confidence.

Interview Practicum

Scenario. A randomized regional rollout estimates a 30% reduction in account takeover, but the 95% interval ranges from a 69% reduction to a 58% increase. Challenge abandonment rises by 0.42 percentage points with a narrow interval. The CISO asks for a red/green recommendation before tomorrow’s board meeting.

Prompt. Write and present the recommendation.

Strong candidates ask about absolute event counts, assignment unit, outcome maturity, missing linkage, spillover, minimum worthwhile benefit, maximum tolerable harm, reversibility, affected segments, support capacity, and what the board actually decides.

Then add: the board deck permits one slide, and the control vendor publicly claims an 80% reduction based on a different customer population.

Worked Answer

My headline would be amber with words, not an unexplained color: “Evidence of security benefit is promising but inconclusive; user friction is established. Recommend constrained expansion, not global deployment.” The slide shows the absolute risk difference and interval against benefit and harm thresholds, takeover counts and denominators, the +0.42 pp abandonment effect, and the staged option.

I would not describe the confidence interval as a probability of benefit. If a Bayesian probability is requested, I would fit and document an appropriate model, show prior sensitivity, and keep it separate from the randomized estimate. The vendor’s 80% claim belongs in external evidence with population, implementation, and design differences; it does not replace our result.

The recommendation is a six-week expansion in reversible managed-tenant flows, from 5% to 15%, with no expansion into accessibility-sensitive or high-lockout-risk flows. Stop on abandonment above +0.6 pp, a confirmed severe lockout, telemetry coverage below the approved threshold, or support saturation. Predefine the security-effect gate using mature outcomes and state how spillover will be monitored.

The memo ledger distinguishes observed counts, estimated effects, spillover assumptions, external vendor evidence, and the recommendation. The decision record names the CISO as policy approver, product as user-impact owner, security analytics as estimate owner, and operations as rollout owner. Doing nothing remains an option with its own expected compromise cost; delay is not presented as neutral.

For one slide, I would link an evidence ID to the full memo and technical appendix. If anyone removes the interval or denominator, analytical sign-off is invalidated.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

Is amber evasive? It is evasive without an action. Here it is paired with a specific staged recommendation, stop rules, and decision date.

Why not say the experiment failed? It did not precisely estimate the rare security outcome, but it successfully identified friction and narrowed plausible effects. “Failed” conflates evidence with a preferred conclusion.

How do you choose the next sample size? Base it on a decision-relevant effect and clustered event rate, then account for outcome delay, attrition, sequential rules, and operational feasibility—not an arbitrary power target alone.

Should executives see posterior distributions? Only if the model and interpretation aid the decision. Often an effect plot, consequence table, and sensitivity summary communicate better; keep technical detail available.

What is Principal-level communication? It refuses precision theater while still making a choice possible, exposes value judgments, protects dissent and provenance, and designs the next reversible decision rather than merely requesting more data.

Key Takeaways

  • A decision memo separates observed facts, estimates, assumptions, external evidence, and recommendations.
  • Inconclusive evidence can still rule out options, establish costs, or justify a reversible stage.
  • Uncertainty visuals need decision thresholds, intervals, raw counts, denominators, and mature outcomes.
  • Forecast registers turn implicit beliefs into resolvable probabilities and reveal overconfidence over time.
  • Recommendations should define authority, population, duration, guardrails, owners, and transitions.

Field lesson: use a two-column executive decision

The sec-pr decision brief placed platform readiness beside detector effectiveness instead of blending them into one status. Verified engineering evidence supported controlled use of the validation platform; missing adjudicated labels, confirmed misses, representative cohorts, and observation time kept effectiveness claims gated.
