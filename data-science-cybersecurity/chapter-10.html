Chapter 10: Cyber Risk Is a Property of a Connected System

The request sounds reasonable: “Give leadership one enterprise cyber-risk score.” It is also underspecified. Risk to which assets, from which attack paths, during what horizon, under which controls, and measured with what evidence? A scalar can summarize a model after those questions are answered. It cannot answer them.

This chapter constructs a measurable system for Meridian, a software company whose customer-data service can be reached through workforce identities, CI/CD, cloud administration, and an AI support agent. The organizing artifact is a typed graph. Assets carry consequence; identities exercise authority; trust boundaries constrain movement; attack steps express adversary capability; controls interrupt mechanisms; telemetry makes states observable. Risk is computed over paths, then preserved as drill-down evidence rather than crushed into an unexplained color.

Start with Consequence-Bearing Assets

An inventory becomes a risk model only when nodes have operational meaning. Meridian uses five node types:

Asset:
  keys: [asset_id, environment]
  fields: [mission, data_class, outage_cost_per_hour, integrity_impact, owner]
Identity:
  keys: [principal_id, issuer]
  fields: [kind, privilege, auth_strength, lifecycle_state, owner]
Boundary:
  keys: [boundary_id]
  fields: [source_zone, destination_zone, allowed_protocols, policy_version]
AttackStep:
  keys: [step_id]
  fields: [technique_id, preconditions, success_probability, evidence_ids]
Control:
  keys: [control_id, version]
  fields: [mechanism, coverage, conditional_effect, failure_mode, evidence_grade]

Edges are typed too: AUTHENTICATES_TO, CAN_ASSUME, DEPLOYS_TO, READS, CROSSES, ENABLES, BLOCKS, and OBSERVED_BY. A shared administrator account is not merely an identity row; its CAN_ASSUME edges expose how one compromise fans out. A trust boundary is not a rectangle on a diagram; it is a policy-enforced transition with a version and observable decisions.

Consequence belongs primarily to assets. Threat is an actor's intent and capability. Vulnerability is a weakness. Exposure is reachability under current configuration. Likelihood is a model over attack attempts and control behavior. Impact is the conditional consequence of a successful path. Mixing these nouns into one “severity” field destroys causal meaning.

Events, Alerts, Incidents, Losses, and Near Misses

These records occupy different levels:

  • an event is a state change or observation;
  • an alert is a detector's assertion requiring action;
  • an incident is an adjudicated harmful security episode;
  • a loss is realized consequence such as outage, disclosure, recovery cost, or trust damage;
  • a near miss is a path that progressed but was interrupted before consequence.

Ten alerts may describe one incident; one incident may produce several losses; a control can create near misses by stopping attacks. Counts cannot be freely divided. Meridian gives each table explicit keys and relations: alert.event_ids[], incident.alert_ids[], incident.asset_ids[], loss.incident_id, and near_miss.interrupting_control_id.

A Path Model with Causal Semantics

For path $p=(s_1,\ldots,s_k)$ to asset $a$, a first approximation is

$$R_p=P(A_p)\left[\prod_{j=1}^k P(s_j\mid pa(s_j),do(C=c))\right]I_a.$$

$P(A_p)$ is attempted-path frequency, $pa(s_j)$ are prerequisites, $C$ represents controls, and $I_a$ is consequence. The do notation is a warning: observed attacks under a control do not automatically identify what would happen without it. Control effectiveness needs experiments, quasi-experiments, engineering tests, or explicit assumptions.

Paths are not independent. The same stolen token may enable several branches, while a common identity provider failure disables multiple controls. A Bayesian network, fault tree, or Monte Carlo structural model can retain shared causes. Summing path risks is acceptable only when overlap is negligible or corrected.

For a control $c$, Meridian records conditional mechanism effectiveness rather than a universal percentage:

$$e_c(z)=1-P(S=1\mid do(c=1),Z=z)/P(S=1\mid do(c=0),Z=z).$$

MFA may strongly reduce password-only compromise yet do little against stolen sessions. Coverage and effectiveness are separate: a 95-percent-effective control deployed to 40 percent of privileged identities does not provide 95-percent enterprise protection.

ATT&CK and ATLAS Are Semantic Coordinates

MITRE ATT&CK maps enterprise adversary behavior. ATLAS maps threats to AI-enabled systems. They are not probability tables. Meridian attaches technique identifiers to attack-step nodes and data components or telemetry sources to OBSERVED_BY edges.

Examples:

The Meridian Attack-and-Control Graph

flowchart LR
    X((External actor)) --> P[Phished developer]
    P --> T[Stolen session]
    T --> G{Source control boundary}
    G --> W[Workflow modification]
    W --> B[Build identity]
    B --> R{Cloud deploy boundary}
    R --> D[(Customer-data service)]
    K[Phishing-resistant reauth] -. interrupts .-> T
    V[Signed workflow policy] -. interrupts .-> W
    H[Workload identity attestation] -. interrupts .-> B
    M[Runtime egress policy] -. limits impact .-> D
    T --> E1[[Identity telemetry]]
    W --> E2[[Repository audit]]
    B --> E3[[Cloud audit]]

Diamonds are trust transitions, rectangles are adversary states or identities, the oval is the actor, the cylinder-like node is the consequence-bearing asset, dotted edges are controls, and double brackets are evidence. This topology differs from a pipeline: several controls act on distinct mechanisms, and evidence nodes observe rather than cause the path.

The graph exposes dependencies. Reauthentication and repository approval both depend on the identity provider; treating them as independent overstates defense in depth. Build attestation and cloud audit may share the same logging plane; an outage removes both enforcement evidence and measurement. Such common causes become explicit parent nodes in the computational graph.

From NIST CSF 2.0 to Measures

CSF functions organize outcomes; they do not prescribe metrics. Meridian maps them to graph operations:

  • Govern: ownership, risk tolerance, evidence acceptance, exception expiry.
  • Identify: asset consequence and identity-edge completeness.
  • Protect: control coverage at attack transitions.
  • Detect: probability and delay of observing step progression.
  • Respond: probability of interrupting a live path before consequence.
  • Recover: time and residual loss after asset impact.

Each measure has a denominator. “MFA coverage” becomes privileged identity-days protected by phishing-resistant authentication divided by eligible privileged identity-days. “Detection coverage” becomes tested attack-step instances observed within a specified delay divided by valid test instances, sliced by technique and telemetry state.

Leading indicators describe precursors such as exposed privileged edges. Operational indicators describe control execution, latency, and failure. Lagging indicators describe incidents and losses. Outcome indicators estimate consequence or avoided consequence. A balanced view retains all four; a composite must never erase the components.

Why One Score Usually Fails

Suppose leadership insists on $R=\sum_a w_a E[L_a]$. The formula is defensible only if asset loss distributions, weights, horizon, dependence, and uncertainty are visible. Meridian presents an expected-loss range plus top contributing paths and sensitivity. It refuses ordinal multiplication such as likelihood 4 × impact 5 = risk 20: category numbers lack ratio-scale meaning.

Monte Carlo simulation is useful when parameters are distributions. Draw attack frequency, step success, control availability, and impact; propagate them through the graph; report median, tail quantiles, and rank stability. Sensitivity analysis answers which uncertain parameter could reverse a funding decision. That is more actionable than adding decimal places to a score.

Graph Lab: Remove a Control, Recompute Reachability

The lab uses only Python's standard library. It enumerates simple attacker-to-asset paths, applies control reductions at specific edges, and reports expected loss. Shared controls are represented once per edge; this small fixture assumes path independence and says so.

Save as ch10_graph_lab.py and execute with the book .venv.

from collections import defaultdict

edges = {
    ("actor", "dev_session"): (0.08, "reauth"),
    ("dev_session", "repo_write"): (0.30, "branch_policy"),
    ("repo_write", "build_id"): (0.40, "attestation"),
    ("build_id", "customer_data"): (0.25, "deploy_gate"),
    ("actor", "support_agent"): (0.05, "corpus_signing"),
    ("support_agent", "customer_data"): (0.10, "tool_scope"),
}
effect = {"reauth": .75, "branch_policy": .80, "attestation": .60,
          "deploy_gate": .70, "corpus_signing": .50, "tool_scope": .65}
impact = {"customer_data": 2_000_000}

adj = defaultdict(list)
for (u, v), (p, c) in edges.items():
    adj[u].append((v, p, c))

def paths(node, target, seen=()):
    if node == target:
        yield []
    for v, p, c in adj[node]:
        if v not in seen:
            for tail in paths(v, target, seen + (node,)):
                yield [(node, v, p, c)] + tail

def risk(disabled=frozenset()):
    rows = []
    for path in paths("actor", "customer_data"):
        probability = 1.0
        for _, _, p, control in path:
            reduction = 0 if control in disabled else effect[control]
            probability *= p * (1 - reduction)
        names = "->".join([path[0][0]] + [e[1] for e in path])
        rows.append((names, probability, probability * impact["customer_data"]))
    return rows

for label, disabled in [("baseline", frozenset()),
                        ("no_branch_policy", frozenset({"branch_policy"}))]:
    rows = risk(disabled)
    print(label)
    for name, probability, loss in rows:
        print(f"  {name}: p={probability:.8f} expected_loss=${loss:,.2f}")
    print(f"  total=${sum(r[2] for r in rows):,.2f}")

  actor->support_agent->customer_data: p=0.00087500 expected_loss=$1,750.00
  total=$1,778.80

The AI-support path dominates despite lower raw attempt probability because its remaining controls are weaker. Removing branch policy multiplies the software-supply path by five, but the enterprise total rises only 12.7 percent. This is exactly why totals need path drill-down: a critical control can look small inside aggregate loss.

For production, replace point probabilities with distributions, represent common-cause nodes, attach evidence provenance, prevent cycles or use a dynamic model, and validate topology with service owners and red teams. “No path” can mean missing inventory, not safety.

Operating the Model

Graph updates are governed changes. Asset owners approve consequence models. Identity teams own authority edges. Control owners attest versions and coverage. Detection teams own observation mappings. Risk analysts own assumptions and uncertainty, but cannot invent missing system relationships.

Every quarterly review asks: which high-consequence nodes lack owners; which trust crossings have no enforced decision; which attack steps lack telemetry; which controls share failure domains; which parameter drives decision sensitivity; and which topology changes occurred since the last model? A signed snapshot makes historical comparisons possible.

Near misses validate portions of topology. If an attack reached repo_write and branch policy stopped it, the path prefix is observed and the control executed. It does not identify the probability of eventual data loss. Purple-team tests validate reachability and observability under controlled conditions but must not be treated as unbiased attacker frequency.

Calibrating and Challenging the Graph

Every parameter carries a provenance class: incident frequency, controlled test, production control test, external study, or expert elicitation. These answer different questions. A purple-team success estimates capability in a designed scenario, not annual frequency. Incident counts reflect deployed controls and observation. External rates require transport assumptions. Expert ranges should be elicited independently before discussion to reduce anchoring.

Calibration compares predicted frequencies with mature observations at graph cuts where evidence is credible. If the model predicts ten trust-boundary crossings but telemetry sees one, probability error is only one explanation; missing observation or wrong topology may dominate. Challenge sensor sensitivity before tuning probabilities downward.

Graph assurance uses counterexample hunts. Ask owners to name absent access paths; ask red teams to reach an asset without the modeled boundary; ask identity engineers for dormant principals retaining edges; ask recovery teams which dependencies appear only during failover. Version every discovered edge and recompute prior decisions. A stable total with radically changed path attribution still demands review.

Tail consequence needs separate reporting. Expected loss can favor a policy that lowers routine loss while increasing catastrophic exposure. Report exceedance probability $P(L>10\text{ million})$, conditional tail expectation, and the worst credible path. Risk appetite may constrain the tail even when the mean improves.

Time also belongs in the graph. A path requiring four steps may be harmless if defenders reliably interrupt it within minutes, yet dangerous during an identity-provider outage. Dynamic edges therefore carry dwell-time and detection-delay distributions. Simulation compares time to adversary completion with time to observation, decision, and containment. This converts vague “response speed” into a race conditioned on technique and operating state.

Model scope must be printed on every output: included assets, excluded environments, snapshot time, horizon, currency basis, and evidence cutoff. Without that footer, a locally valid graph will be mistaken for an enterprise truth.

Interview Practicum

The prompt is a negotiation: “The board wants one cyber-risk score next month. Design it.” The interviewer plays three stakeholders. The CFO supplies outage cost but distrusts probabilities. The CISO wants ATT&CK coverage. The platform VP says the asset inventory is stale.

The candidate gets credit for drawing a typed graph before proposing arithmetic. They define horizon and consequence, identify crown-jewel assets, authority paths, trust boundaries, attack transitions, controls, and observations. They offer an expected-loss distribution with drill-down, not a magic scalar.

At minute ten, the interviewer reveals two controls depend on the same identity provider. The candidate adds a common-cause node and retracts any independence assumption. At minute twenty, the CFO asks to multiply red/amber/green ratings. The candidate explains why ordinal categories cannot support multiplication and proposes ranges with sensitivity. Finally, the platform VP admits inventory coverage is 70 percent. The candidate reports model coverage as a first-class uncertainty and refuses to label absent paths safe.

Worked Answer

Deliver a bounded first model for two material services, not an invented enterprise total. Define asset-specific loss distributions over twelve months. Build identity and deployment reachability from authoritative configuration, then validate trust crossings with owners. Map plausible attack steps to ATT&CK or ATLAS and local scenario identifiers. Attach controls to mechanisms and telemetry to observations.

Compute path-conditioned loss with explicit dependence and parameter grades. Present median and tail risk, top paths, control-removal deltas, missing-topology coverage, and sensitivity. Use CSF functions as governance and outcome groupings. The board sees a concise range and movement; engineers retain graph-level evidence.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

How do you combine confidentiality and availability impact? Keep separate consequence dimensions until an accountable owner supplies a common monetary or utility model. Do not average incompatible ordinal ratings.

Does ATT&CK coverage equal security? No. It measures mapped behavioral scope. It says nothing by itself about attacker frequency, sensor quality, control effect, or consequence.

How should unknown probabilities be handled? Use ranges or distributions tied to evidence grades, then run sensitivity. If a decision reverses across plausible values, fund measurement before claiming a ranking.

What invalidates the graph? Unowned high-impact assets, stale authority edges, hidden shared dependencies, topology changes without versioning, or outputs that cannot be traced to evidence.

Key Takeaways

  • Risk emerges from connected assets, authority, trust transitions, attack mechanisms, controls, and consequences.
  • ATT&CK and ATLAS supply behavioral coordinates, not probabilities or proof of coverage.
  • Control coverage, conditional effect, availability, and common-cause failure are separate quantities.
  • Events, alerts, incidents, near misses, and losses require distinct keys and denominators.
  • A scalar is acceptable only as a view over a versioned, inspectable graph with uncertainty.
  • The most valuable output is often the path or assumption that can change a decision.
