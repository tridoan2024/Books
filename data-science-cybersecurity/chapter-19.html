Chapter 19: Metrics That Survive Contact with Reality

A detector can have a beautiful ROC curve and still be unusable. At one malicious event per million, a false-positive rate of 0.1 percent creates roughly one thousand false alerts for each million events. Even perfect recall would yield precision near 0.1 percent. The model is not “99.9 percent accurate” in any operationally useful sense; it has produced a queue no team can investigate.

Security metrics must retain their meaning when prevalence changes, labels mature late, attacks cluster, and analyst capacity is finite. The evaluation object is not merely a classifier. It is a decision policy operating on a population, at a threshold, with an intervention and a queue.

Reconstruct Counts Before Rates

For true positives \(TP\), false positives \(FP\), false negatives \(FN\), and true negatives \(TN\):

\[ \text{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad \text{recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad \text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}. \]

Given prevalence \(\pi=P(Y=1)\), true-positive rate \(t\), and false-positive rate \(f\), expected precision is

\[ \frac{t\pi}{t\pi+f(1-\pi)}. \]

With \(\pi=10^{-6}\), \(t=0.8\), and \(f=10^{-3}\), precision is about 0.0008: eight true alerts per ten thousand alerts. Report expected counts per day or million events alongside every rate. Counts expose capacity immediately.

Define the denominator carefully. Event-level FPR may look tiny while identity-level false alert rate is high because active users contribute thousands of events. Evaluate at the unit receiving an action: message, session, identity-day, host, incident, or campaign. Deduplicate correlated alerts before claiming analyst burden.

PR Curves, ROC Curves, and Thresholds

ROC curves plot TPR against FPR and are useful for comparing ranking behavior across all thresholds. Under extreme imbalance, their large true-negative denominator can visually minimize operationally disastrous false positives. Precision-recall curves focus on positive retrieval and reflect prevalence; their no-skill precision baseline is \(\pi\).

Average precision is not a deployment decision. It aggregates thresholds, many of which the team cannot use. Report performance at feasible queue sizes and interventions. “Recall at 200 identity investigations per day” is often more meaningful than area under a curve.

PR curves are prevalence-dependent. A test set enriched to 10 percent attacks cannot supply production precision without reweighting. If class-conditional score distributions transfer, adjust using deployment prevalence. Better, construct a representative temporal test set and report uncertainty in prevalence.

Calibration Is Decision Reliability

A calibrated score means that among predictions near \(p), about fraction \(p\) are positive. Calibration supports cost-sensitive actions and comparison across segments, but aggregate calibration can conceal subgroup error. Use reliability diagrams, Brier score,

\[ \text{Brier}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(p_i-y_i)^2, \]

and expected calibration error with disclosed binning. ECE can be gamed by coarse bins and should never stand alone.

Calibrate on data later than training and earlier than final testing. Isotonic regression is flexible but can overfit small samples; Platt scaling imposes a sigmoid; beta calibration can handle asymmetric distortions. After prevalence or policy changes, prior correction may be appropriate, but attacker adaptation can change more than the prior.

Do not call arbitrary anomaly scores probabilities. If outcomes are censored or selectively reviewed, the calibration target is also biased. Maintain exploration samples and delayed-outcome reconciliation.

Utility Under Capacity Constraints

Let action \(a\) have utility conditional on outcome. A simple threshold chooses action when

\[ p(x)C_{FN} > (1-p(x))C_{FP}+C_A, \]

where \(C_{FN}\) is avoided missed-attack loss, \(C_{FP}\) is false-intervention cost, and \(C_A\) is action cost. The implied threshold is

\[ p(x)>\frac{C_{FP}+C_A}{C_{FN}+C_{FP}}. \]

This is only valid if probabilities are calibrated and costs are relevant to the unit. Security losses are heavy-tailed and uncertain; present sensitivity across plausible costs rather than one theatrical dollar value.

Capacity turns thresholding into constrained selection. If analysts can review \(K\) cases, rank by expected marginal utility and take positive-utility items up to \(K\). Diversity constraints may prevent one campaign or tenant from consuming the queue. Service-time differences matter: ten complex cloud investigations are not equivalent to ten automated URL checks.

Time Metrics Need State Semantics

Time-to-detect begins at attack occurrence, often unknown—not at log ingestion. Time-to-triage begins when actionable evidence is available, not when a ticket is eventually created. Time-to-contain ends when attacker capability is removed, not when an analyst clicks “resolved.” Define timestamps and censor unresolved cases.

Medians hide tails; means are dominated by rare long cases. Report quantiles and survival curves. Separate processing latency, queue latency, investigation time, and remediation time. A detector that fires earlier but creates a large queue may increase end-to-end containment time.

Coverage is also conditional. ATT&CK technique coverage based on a rule list measures declared scope, not observable or validated coverage. Distinguish telemetry coverage, logic coverage, validation coverage, and response coverage. Weight by exposure only with an explicit, reviewable threat model.

Safe Lab: Compare Two Queue Policies

The synthetic fixture evaluates detectors at a daily capacity of four alerts and assigns utility: +100 for a true investigated attack, -4 for a false investigation, and -35 for every missed attack.

records = [
    # id, truth, detector_a, detector_b
    ("e1", 1, .99, .70), ("e2", 1, .80, .95),
    ("e3", 1, .45, .90), ("e4", 0, .90, .30),
    ("e5", 0, .85, .20), ("e6", 0, .70, .80),
    ("e7", 0, .60, .10), ("e8", 0, .55, .75),
    ("e9", 0, .40, .60), ("e10", 0, .30, .50),
]

def evaluate(rows, score_index, capacity=4):
    selected = sorted(rows, key=lambda r: r[score_index], reverse=True)[:capacity]
    chosen = {r[0] for r in selected}
    tp = sum(r[1] for r in selected)
    fp = capacity - tp
    positives = sum(r[1] for r in rows)
    fn = positives - tp
    utility = 100 * tp - 4 * fp - 35 * fn
    return {"tp": tp, "fp": fp, "fn": fn,
            "precision": tp / capacity, "recall": tp / positives,
            "utility": utility, "selected": sorted(chosen)}

a = evaluate(records, 2)
b = evaluate(records, 3)
assert a == {"tp": 2, "fp": 2, "fn": 1, "precision": .5,
             "recall": 2/3, "utility": 157, "selected": ["e1","e2","e4","e5"]}
assert b == {"tp": 2, "fp": 2, "fn": 1, "precision": .5,
             "recall": 2/3, "utility": 157, "selected": ["e2","e3","e6","e8"]}
print(a["utility"], b["utility"], a["selected"], b["selected"])

Expected output:

157 157 ['e1', 'e2', 'e4', 'e5'] ['e2', 'e3', 'e6', 'e8']

Aggregate metrics and utility tie, but the policies catch different attacks. Detector A finds e1; B finds e3. This matters if e3 is a novel campaign or higher-impact asset. Evaluation should add attack-family and exposure slices, then test uncertainty. A tie is not permission to ignore composition.

Uncertainty for Rare Events

Events sharing a campaign, identity, host, or time window are not independent. A binomial confidence interval over alerts can be far too narrow. Bootstrap at the cluster that could recur independently—often campaign or incident—and use temporal blocks when conditions drift.

For precision from a probability sample of reviewed alerts, Wilson or Bayesian beta-binomial intervals are more stable than normal approximations. If review is stratified by score or segment, weight observations by inverse inclusion probability. Report the number of confirmed positives, unresolved cases, sampling design, and maturity window.

Comparing two detectors on the same events calls for paired analysis. Bootstrap paired clusters or compare discordant outcomes; do not construct independent intervals and infer significance from overlap. For operational utility, propagate uncertainty in prevalence, labels, and cost as well as model performance.

Rare catastrophic outcomes may not support a narrow interval at all. State an upper bound or scenario range and identify what additional evidence would change the decision.

Metric Schema for Reproducibility

Every reported metric should carry its contract:

metric_id: identity_detection.precision_at_capacity
version: 3
unit: identity_day
population: managed_identities_with_complete_auth_telemetry
score_version: detector_b_2026_08_14
selection: top_200_per_utc_day
label: confirmed_compromise_within_60d
unresolved_policy: censored
deduplication: one_alert_per_identity_per_24h
interval: cluster_bootstrap_by_incident_2000_replicates
slices: [tenant_tier, auth_protocol, region]

Without this context, a number cannot be compared over time. A falling alert rate after deduplication is not a detection improvement; a precision increase after narrowing the population may not improve total attacks found.

Transport Metrics Across Prevalence and Time

Security teams routinely evaluate on a challenge set whose prevalence differs from deployment. If sensitivity \(t\) and specificity \(1-f\) are stable, precision at a new prevalence \(\pi'\) can be recomputed as

\[ PPV'=\frac{t\pi'}{t\pi'+f(1-\pi')}. \]

This transport is narrower than it appears. It assumes the distribution within positive and negative classes remains sufficiently stable. A new authentication protocol, tenant population, or attacker technique can change score distributions and invalidate both \(t\) and \(f\). Report the assumption and verify it with fresh labeled slices rather than applying prior correction indefinitely.

Thresholds also interact with arrival bursts. A policy that fits average daily capacity can fail during a campaign. Model the queue: arrivals have time-varying rate \(\lambda(t)\), analysts have service-time distribution rather than a single rate, and unresolved work carries over. Evaluate the probability that queue age exceeds the response objective, not only alerts per day. Reserve capacity for high-severity cases and define load shedding: aggregation, automated enrichment, temporary threshold changes, or escalation to incident mode. Silent dropping is not a capacity policy.

Metrics should measure incremental value over a baseline. For a new detector, compare attacks found uniquely, time gained, and analyst work displaced relative to existing rules. If it produces true alerts already found earlier by another sensor, standalone recall exaggerates portfolio value. Attribute shared detections transparently—first touch, Shapley-style scenario analysis, or set-based overlap—without pretending one convention is causal truth.

Decision-curve analysis can show net benefit across threshold probabilities, but it inherits cost and calibration assumptions. Present a threshold range over which the detector improves on “investigate none” and the current policy. A narrow region signals fragility. Stress it under lower prevalence, worse label sensitivity, higher service time, and adversarial concentration. A production metric survives contact with reality only if its conclusion remains useful across plausible conditions or clearly identifies the condition that reverses it.

Finally, protect against survivorship in incident recall. “Detected incidents” excludes attacks never discovered. Use independent sources—red-team exercises, customer reports, threat hunting, post-incident retrospectives, and sampled forensic review—to estimate discovery gaps. Call the metric recall on known incidents unless the denominator genuinely represents all incidents.

Choosing a Primary Metric

The primary metric should correspond to the deployment decision. For a fixed analyst team, use expected net benefit or incident recall at a workload and latency constraint. For an automated block, use prevented harmful actions with strict false-block and bypass guardrails. For ranking-only assistance, measure lift in the first \(K\) reviewed cases and time saved relative to analyst ordering. Always pair the primary metric with coverage and data-quality checks so improvement cannot be purchased by silently excluding difficult traffic.

Pre-register the threshold, population, maturity window, deduplication, and stopping rule before examining the final test set. Repeatedly selecting the best slice or threshold creates multiple-comparison optimism even without formal model training. If many detectors or segments are compared, use a held-out confirmation period and distinguish exploratory findings from decision evidence.

Production Failure Analysis

Enriched test prevalence. Precision from a balanced benchmark is reported as production precision. Reweight or use representative data.

Event-level inflation. One incident produces 500 true-positive alerts. Deduplicate to the response unit and report incident recall.

Selective labels. Only high scores are investigated, making low scores appear negative. Preserve exploration review.

Label delay. Recent alerts look precise because false negatives have not matured. Use fixed maturity windows and censoring.

Capacity omission. A threshold is tuned without queue service times or staffing. Simulate arrival and service distributions and define overload behavior.

Segment masking. Aggregate calibration hides failure on legacy authentication or small tenants. Publish minimum-support slices and shrink noisy estimates rather than hiding them.

Metric gaming. Analysts close easy tickets to improve time-to-triage. Measure state transitions from immutable event timestamps and pair speed with quality and reopen rates.

Adaptive displacement. Precision remains high while attackers move to uncovered protocols. Track exposure and telemetry coverage, not only observed alerts.

Interview Practicum

Scenario: One Attack per Million.

Prompt

Compare two detectors when attacks occur once per million events. Detector A has 90 percent recall and 0.1 percent FPR; Detector B has 60 percent recall and 0.001 percent FPR. The SOC can review 100 alerts daily across 20 million events.

Worked Answer

Start with counts. Expect 20 attacks daily. A yields 18 true alerts and about 19,980 false alerts, so its raw policy is impossible. B yields 12 true alerts and about 200 false alerts, still above capacity but far closer. Do not choose solely from these summary rates: inspect score distributions and calculate recall at the 100-alert constraint, deduplicated to investigation units. Estimate precision and recall with a representative temporal sample, cluster intervals by campaign, and value missed attacks by exposure.

Calibrate if scores will drive different interventions. Allocate some capacity to random exploration so low-score performance remains measurable. Report attack-family recall, queue latency, and uncovered population.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

Constraint Change

The interviewer then says Detector A uniquely catches cloud-token theft, while B catches commodity password spray. Token theft is rare but has ten times the expected impact.

The problem becomes portfolio selection. Reserve capacity or use expected-loss ranking across detector outputs, deduplicate overlap, and apply diversity constraints so commodity volume cannot displace high-impact token cases. Validate the tenfold impact assumption with ranges. A detector dominated in aggregate can be essential for a high-value slice.

Weak Answers and Probes

Accuracy is meaningless here. AUROC does not express queue feasibility. F1 silently assigns a particular symmetry to precision and recall and ignores service capacity. Selecting B from lower FPR alone ignores score thresholds and attack composition.

If labels cover only reviewed alerts, introduce probability-sampled review and inverse-probability estimation. If leadership demands one number, provide expected net benefit at stated capacity with a range, plus mandatory recall and burden guardrails. Thirty-second answer: translate rates into counts, evaluate at capacity and action unit, calibrate for costs, and expose uncertainty and segment tradeoffs.

Key Takeaways

  • Translate every rare-event rate into expected operational counts.
  • Evaluate at the unit that receives an action and deduplicate correlated alerts.
  • PR views expose imbalance, but deployment still requires a feasible threshold.
  • Calibration, utility, and capacity answer different questions and all matter.
  • Confidence intervals must respect campaign, identity, and temporal dependence.
  • A detector portfolio may be valuable because of complementary high-impact coverage even when aggregate metrics tie.
