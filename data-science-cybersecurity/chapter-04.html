Chapter 4: Data Preparation, Exploration, and Feature Engineering

This chapter is a forensic notebook. A login-risk model scores 0.97 AUC offline and 0.61 after deployment. Rather than tune the model, we freeze three login times, reconstruct exactly what the system knew, and test every feature against an availability clock. The culprit is not an algorithm. It is a future fact disguised as a column.

Notebook Setup: Three Clocks, Never One

An authentication record has an event time: when the login occurred. A source fact has an availability time: when the feature pipeline could read it. An outcome has a label time: when compromise was adjudicated. Point-in-time correctness requires that features for decision time (t) use facts whose availability time is at or before (t), while labels may mature later.

The raw fixture contains login attempts, device observations, password resets, case outcomes, and ingestion timestamps. The unit is one login attempt. The target is confirmed compromise within fourteen days. The model decision occurs immediately after authentication evidence arrives.

login_id  principal  event_time        decision_time
L1        u1         Aug-01 09:00      Aug-01 09:00:02
L2        u1         Aug-03 10:00      Aug-03 10:00:02
L3        u2         Aug-03 11:00      Aug-03 11:00:02

A password-reset record for u1 occurred after L2 but was backfilled into a table joined by principal. A case system later set reset_after_incident=1. The training query reads the latest principal row without an as-of predicate, so both L1 and L2 inherit the future value. The model learns the investigation workflow.

Cell 1: Profile Before Repair

Profiling is not a ritual of printing describe(). It tests claims needed by the analysis:

  • Are login IDs unique, and are source IDs stable across replays?
  • What fraction of events arrive after the online latency budget?
  • Which columns change after initial ingestion?
  • Are missing values concentrated by source, tenant, platform, or attack period?
  • Do outcome dates precede adjudication dates?
  • Are counts physically possible and timestamps ordered?
  • Does one principal appear in train and validation?

For numeric features, inspect quantiles, zero mass, tail behavior, and exposure. For categories, inspect cardinality, unseen values, and rare levels. For time, inspect gaps, lateness, seasonality, and release boundaries. For joins, measure unmatched, one-to-many, and multiplicative rows. A join that grows 100,000 logins into 128,000 rows is not “more data”; it may be duplicated evidence.

Exploration should have a question ledger. Plotting hundreds of correlations and selecting an exciting one invites data dredging. Mark exploratory discoveries and verify them on untouched time periods.

Cell 2: Classify Missingness as a Mechanism

Let (R=1) mean a value is observed and (X) the complete-data values.

MCAR means (P(R\mid X)=P(R)): missingness is unrelated to observed or unobserved values. A random packet drop may approximate it, though real outages often do not.

MAR means missingness depends on observed data but, conditional on them, not the missing value. Device posture may be absent more often on a known legacy platform; conditioning on platform may support an MAR analysis.

MNAR means missingness depends on the unseen value even after observed variables. Attackers disabling an agent specifically when executing malicious code creates adversarial MNAR. No generic imputer solves identification under MNAR; use sensitivity analysis, alternate sensors, and explicit missingness features where safe.

Missing has domain meanings: “not applicable,” “sensor unsupported,” “collector late,” “redacted,” “lookup found no match,” and “true zero” are different states. Collapsing them into null destroys information. An absent reputation lookup can become predictive because the service fails during attacks; deploying that feature may encode an outage rather than threat.

Do not globally fill numeric nulls before splitting. Fit imputation statistics on training data and apply them unchanged to validation and test. Add missing indicators when the mechanism is stable and legitimate, but monitor them for source-policy changes and attacker manipulation.

Cell 3: Separate Errors, Outliers, and Rare Truth

A negative duration is usually an error. A login from 20,000 km away may be a geolocation error, VPN, shared account, or actual compromise. Rare is not synonymous with malicious, and malicious is not synonymous with statistically rare.

Validation rules should distinguish impossible values from improbable ones. Quarantine impossible rows or repair them from authoritative sources with provenance. Preserve plausible extremes and use robust transforms or models. Winsorizing attack bursts because they are outliers deletes the phenomenon the model should learn.

For skewed counts, log1p compresses scale while retaining zeros. Standardization is useful for distance- and gradient-based models; trees generally do not require it. Fit scalers only on training data. An ordinal encoder needs explicit order. A nominal encoder needs unknown-category behavior. Frequency encoding can leak target-period composition when computed globally.

Cell 4: Deduplicate and Resolve Entities

Deduplication asks whether records represent the same event; entity resolution asks whether identifiers represent the same entity. They require different evidence.

Exact event deduplication may use a source event ID plus producer. If IDs are absent, construct a fingerprint from stable fields, but account for legitimate repeated failures. “Same user, IP, and second” may collapse a retry storm. Keep ingestion lineage and choose a deterministic survivor.

Entity resolution can connect renamed accounts, federated identities, devices, and sessions. Deterministic links using tenant-scoped immutable IDs are preferable. Probabilistic linkage must expose match confidence and avoid cross-tenant collisions. Never let an email address silently become a global person identifier. Split data by resolved entity groups so alternate IDs do not cross train and test.

Cell 5: Put Every Candidate Feature on Trial

A feature is eligible only if it passes five tests:

  1. Availability: Could production obtain it before the decision deadline?
  2. Provenance: Can the value be traced to versioned facts and transformations?
  3. Semantic stability: Will the meaning survive source and policy changes?
  4. Leakage review: Is it caused by the outcome, adjudication, or intervention?
  5. Abuse review: Can an attacker cheaply control or suppress it?

Temporal features

For login at (t), useful features include failures in ((t-1h,t]), distinct devices in ((t-30d,t)), seconds since prior successful login, and ratio of failed attempts to all attempts. Specify boundary inclusion. Including the current outcome field or later events is leakage. A rolling feature must group by entity and shift where the current row should not contribute.

Recency needs a policy for no prior event. Encode “no history” separately from an arbitrary huge duration. Rates retain numerator and denominator or smoothing information. A ratio of one failure over one attempt is less certain than 100 over 100.

Categorical, text, and graph features

Categorical features can use one-hot encoding for bounded vocabularies, hashing for high cardinality, or leakage-safe target encoding fitted out of fold. Text features must exclude notes written after disposition; even document timestamps can be misleading when notes are edited in place. Graph features such as privileged-neighbor count must use the entitlement graph valid and available at decision time. Computing current graph centrality for historical logins leaks future access changes.

Sequence features capture ordering: failure, reset, success differs from success, reset, failure. Aggregates erase this. Dimensionality reduction such as PCA must fit on training only and may reduce interpretability. Feature selection performed on the full dataset leaks validation outcomes. Wrap all learned preprocessing inside the validation procedure.

Cell 6: Point-in-Time Fixture

This runnable fixture contrasts a leaking latest-state join with a correct as-of feature builder.

from datetime import datetime, timezone

def ts(value):
    return datetime.fromisoformat(value).replace(tzinfo=timezone.utc)

logins = [
    {"id": "L1", "user": "u1", "at": ts("2026-08-01T09:00:00")},
    {"id": "L2", "user": "u1", "at": ts("2026-08-03T10:00:00")},
    {"id": "L3", "user": "u2", "at": ts("2026-08-03T11:00:00")},
]
facts = [
    # user, fact, event time, availability time
    ("u1", "device:d1", ts("2026-07-31T12:00:00"), ts("2026-07-31T12:00:05")),
    ("u1", "failure", ts("2026-08-03T09:40:00"), ts("2026-08-03T09:40:03")),
    ("u1", "password_reset", ts("2026-08-03T10:30:00"), ts("2026-08-03T10:30:02")),
    ("u2", "device:d9", ts("2026-08-03T10:00:00"), ts("2026-08-03T12:00:00")),
]

def build(login):
    visible = [f for f in facts if f[0] == login["user"] and f[3] <= login["at"]]
    failures = sum(f[1] == "failure" and f[2] > login["at"].replace(hour=0) for f in visible)
    devices = {f[1] for f in visible if f[1].startswith("device:")}
    reset_seen = any(f[1] == "password_reset" for f in visible)
    return login["id"], failures, len(devices), reset_seen

latest_reset = {user: any(f[1] == "password_reset" for f in facts if f[0] == user)
                for user in {x["user"] for x in logins}}
for login in logins:
    print("as_of", build(login), "leaky_latest_reset", latest_reset[login["user"]])

Expected output:

as_of ('L1', 0, 1, False) leaky_latest_reset True
as_of ('L2', 1, 1, False) leaky_latest_reset True
as_of ('L3', 0, 0, False) leaky_latest_reset False

The reset is unavailable at both u1 login times, yet the latest-state join marks both true. The u2 device occurred earlier but arrived an hour after L3, so it is correctly absent. Event-time filtering alone would still leak it.

Cell 7: Assert the Boundaries

Point-in-time pipelines need executable invariants, not reviewer memory.

from datetime import datetime, timezone

def ts(value):
    return datetime.fromisoformat(value).replace(tzinfo=timezone.utc)

cutoff = ts("2026-08-03T10:00:00")
boundary_facts = [
    ("at_cutoff", cutoff),
    ("one_microsecond_late", ts("2026-08-03T10:00:00.000001")),
]
included = [name for name, available in boundary_facts if available <= cutoff]
assert included == ["at_cutoff"]

train_users = {"u1", "u2"}
validation_users = {"u3"}
assert train_users.isdisjoint(validation_users)

feature_rows = [("L1", False), ("L2", False), ("L3", False)]
assert len(feature_rows) == len({row[0] for row in feature_rows})
assert all(reset_seen is False for _, reset_seen in feature_rows)
print("boundary=closed")
print("entity_split=disjoint")
print("fixture_checks=4 passed")

Expected output:

boundary=closed
entity_split=disjoint
fixture_checks=4 passed

Tests should cover exact cutoff, late corrections, duplicated source IDs, daylight-saving transitions, unknown categories, empty history, source outage, and deterministic replay. Store fixture rows in code review so a semantic change produces an obvious diff.

Cell 8: Choose a Split That Matches Deployment

An IID random split assumes exchangeable rows. Authentication rows violate this when the same user, device, or campaign appears in both sets. A grouped split keeps entities together. A temporal split trains on the past and tests on the future. Often both are needed: train before a cutoff and prevent entity overlap where the deployment claim concerns new entities.

Use three roles. Training fits parameters and preprocessing. Validation selects features, hyperparameters, and thresholds. Test remains sealed for final estimation. Reusing test results for decisions turns the test into validation. With time drift, use rolling-origin evaluation: train through (t), validate on the next interval, advance, and summarize variation across folds.

Labels need a maturity gap. If the target is ATO within fourteen days, stop training examples at least fourteen days before the label snapshot, or mark censoring explicitly. A random split cannot repair immature negatives.

Offline metrics must be recomputed after removing contaminated features. A dramatic decline is evidence that the old score measured future workflow. Compare train and serving feature distributions generated by the same code path. Training-serving skew may arise from different parsers, window boundaries, lookup freshness, or default values.

Cell 9: Missingness and Adversarial Failure Tests

Feature health is part of model behavior. Simulate loss of device telemetry, delayed geolocation, new identity providers, clock skew, and duplicated bursts. Measure predictions and actions, not only null rates. A model that maps missing posture to high risk may lock out a tenant during an agent outage; one that maps it low may create an attacker bypass.

Define explicit degradation: abstain, fall back to a smaller feature set, use a conservative rule, or route to review. Select based on harm. Monitor coverage by cohort, feature age, unknown-category rate, join multiplicity, and point-in-time violations. Alert before outcome metrics mature.

Attacker-induced missingness deserves threat modeling. Can clients omit device fields? Can a proxy delay reputation enrichment past scoring? Can repeated identities poison rolling baselines? Use server-observed signals where possible, cap contributions, retain raw provenance, and test manipulated fixtures.

Notebook Conclusion: Why AUC Fell

The offline model used reset_after_incident, current entitlement centrality, and a device reputation value backfilled hours later. Random splitting placed the same principals and campaign in both partitions. Removing leakage, rebuilding as-of features, grouping principals, and testing on later weeks reduced offline AUC from 0.97 to 0.72. Production AUC of 0.70 now agrees within uncertainty.

The lower score is the successful result. It estimates the deployable problem. The team can improve it honestly through better decision-time instrumentation, sequences, calibrated models, and outcome sampling. Restoring 0.97 by reintroducing future facts would improve only the fiction.

Interview Practicum

Debug the Miraculous Model

Prompt with evidence drops

“A login-risk model has 0.97 offline AUC and 0.61 online. Diagnose it.” The interviewer reveals in stages: password-reset fields are updated after incidents; device intelligence arrives up to two hours late; rows were randomly split; analysts investigate only challenged sessions; and production uses a different null default.

Worked Answer

Strong investigative answer

Freeze model, feature, and source versions and reproduce several production decisions from raw evidence. Build a feature lineage table containing event time, availability time, transformation, and owner. For every candidate feature, compare historical value as originally available with the current backfilled value. Remove post-outcome and post-intervention fields.

Rebuild examples as of login decision time. Add a fourteen-day maturity gap for outcomes. Split temporally and group principals or linked devices. Fit imputation, encoders, scaling, feature selection, and dimensionality reduction inside each training fold. Compare online and offline feature vectors for identical decision IDs.

The different null default is training-serving skew; unify transformation code and test outage behavior. Because only challenged sessions receive labels, observed online performance is selected. Add a randomized audit sample where ethically possible and use sampling weights. Do not claim global recall from investigated traffic alone.

Report a leakage ablation: baseline with all fields, remove reset workflow, remove late intelligence, enforce grouped temporal split, and enforce production defaults. The step changes identify how much optimism each defect contributed. Re-estimate threshold metrics at operational capacity and shadow the repaired pipeline before action.

Worked answer

The principal assertion is: for observation (i) at decision time (t_i), every feature fact (j) must satisfy available_at_j <= t_i; every learned preprocessing artifact must be fit without validation or test rows; and outcome observation must not influence feature construction. Event time alone is insufficient because backfilled data can describe earlier events but become known later.

A safe SQL pattern joins a login to facts on entity plus fact.available_at <= login.decision_at, applies event-window predicates separately, and resolves multiple versions by the latest version available by the cutoff. The test fixture must include a fact whose event precedes login but whose arrival follows it.

Weak answers

  • “Tune regularization” treats a data defect as model variance.
  • “Production drifted” is a hypothesis, not a diagnosis.
  • “Drop all null rows” changes population and hides outages.
  • “Use current tables for more complete history” guarantees backfill leakage.
  • “AUC is still above random” ignores action thresholds and selected labels.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

Follow-up probes with answers

A feature is available before scoring but caused by a prior intervention. Is it leakage? It may be legitimately available yet create policy dependence or treatment leakage. Define the deployment policy and causal pathway; evaluate stability when intervention changes.

How handle an IP reputation correction? Version values with validity and availability. Replay the version known at decision time; use corrected values only for analyses whose estimand permits hindsight.

Can a missingness indicator be useful? Yes when it represents stable decision-time information, but test source changes and manipulation. It does not make MNAR bias disappear.

How detect duplicate leakage across accounts? Group by resolved device, household, organization, campaign, or artifact as appropriate; measure similarity across splits and run stricter sensitivity splits.

Would you restore the old model while rebuilding? Only if its live harm is lower than fallback and contaminated features exist online at decision time without post-outcome dependence. Otherwise fall back to a reviewed rule or abstention policy.

Communication drills

Thirty seconds: “Reconstruct identical production decisions from raw facts, enforce availability-time joins, remove post-incident fields, add label maturity, group entities, split forward in time, and unify preprocessing. The honest offline AUC will fall; agreement with online behavior is the first success.”

Five minutes: Explain the three clocks, lineage audit, leakage ablation, selected-label problem, grouped temporal validation, train-serving parity, fixtures, and safe fallback.

Key Takeaways

  • Event, availability, and label times must be modeled separately.
  • Latest-state joins are unsuitable for historical feature reconstruction.
  • Missingness is a mechanism, sometimes controlled by attackers, not a cleaning inconvenience.
  • Fit every learned transformation inside the training partition.
  • Grouped temporal splits and outcome-maturity gaps approximate deployment better than random rows.
  • A lower leakage-free score is more valuable than a miraculous invalid score.
