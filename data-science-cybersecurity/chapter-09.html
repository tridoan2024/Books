Chapter 9: The Adversary Is in the Dataset

At 08:00 Monday, Northstar's dashboard looks excellent: alerts per thousand logins are down 40 percent, triage is faster, and reviewed-alert precision is at a yearly high. On Friday, fraud operations reports that confirmed account takeovers rose 18 percent. Nothing is contradictory. The dashboard measures what a detector selected for attention. Fraud measures a delayed, incomplete subset of harm. Between them sits an adversary who probes controls, a review process that creates labels, and interventions that change what can be observed.

Security data is generated by a game, not sampled passively from a stable population. This chapter follows Northstar's identity team through that failure and builds an analysis that remains interpretable when attacks are rare, labels selective, outcomes delayed, and attackers adaptive.

The Monday Paradox

Let an attempt have features $X$, latent malicious status $Y$, score $S$, action $A$, review indicator $R$, and observed outcome $O$. Production stores $X,S,A$; $Y$ becomes known mainly when $R=1$ or a later loss is reported. Thus $P(Y=1\mid X,R=1)\ne P(Y=1\mid X)$. Analyst verdicts are not missing at random: review depends on score, rules, queue capacity, customer tier, and many model features. Training on reviewed alerts teaches the old policy's field of view.

Northstar needs two clocks and explicit unresolved states:

auth_attempt(attempt_id, identity_id, event_time, available_time,
  score_version, risk_score, action, review_probability,
  verdict_time, loss_time, outcome_state, censor_reason)

outcome_state := pending | benign | malicious | censored
censor_reason := horizon_open | account_closed | telemetry_lost

available_time prevents leakage: a day-20 chargeback cannot score a day-0 login. Pending is not benign. Censored means observation ended without resolution.

Rare Events Rewrite Familiar Metrics

Suppose one in 10,000 logins is malicious. A detector with 90 percent sensitivity and 99 percent specificity yields about 90 true positives and 9,999 false positives per million logins:

$$PPV=\frac{0.90(0.0001)}{0.90(0.0001)+0.01(0.9999)}\approx0.0089.$$

Fewer than one alert in a hundred is an attack. An always-benign classifier has 99.99 percent accuracy. Security evaluation instead needs base rate, precision-recall behavior, calibrated risk, capacity, and action costs.

For action $a$, choose

$$a^*(x)=\arg\min_a\sum_yP(Y=y\mid X=x)L(a,y),$$

where $L$ distinguishes allowed takeover, unnecessary challenge, false block, and analyst time. Policy owners approve the loss matrix. A calibrated probability plus explicit costs travels better than a threshold copied between businesses.

Uncertainty dominates rare events. Three malicious cases in 2,000 audited logins is 0.15 percent, but its interval matters more than the point. Use Wilson or exact intervals for simple samples and design-based estimators for stratified samples.

The Label Factory Has a Selection Gate

Northstar labels cases through high-score review, user recovery after challenge, delayed loss reports, and a small random audit of allowed traffic. The first three paths are behaviorally selected. Only the fourth provides controlled visibility below threshold.

Let $\pi_i=P(R_i=1\mid Z_i)$ be the known inclusion probability. A Horvitz-Thompson estimate of malicious total is

$$\widehat T=\sum_{i:R_i=1}\frac{Y_i}{\pi_i},\qquad \widehat p=\widehat T/N.$$

Audit probabilities must be recorded, every selected case pursued, and nonresponse treated separately. Partition allowed traffic into score bands, new versus established identities, and high-impact versus ordinary assets. Give every consequential stratum nonzero coverage; allocate remaining budget toward numerous, uncertain, inexpensive strata. Randomize extra investigation among cases already allowed by policy—not protection versus deliberate harm. Severe cases require shadow decisions, red-team fixtures, natural experiments, or bounds.

Delays, Censoring, and Hidden Counterfactuals

Only mature cohorts belong in a naive binary metric. If 95 percent of fraud reports arrive within 30 days, a seven-day cohort is unresolved, not unusually safe. Comparing it with mature history manufactures improvement.

Let $T$ be time to confirmed compromise and $C$ censoring time. Observe $\tilde T=\min(T,C)$ and $\delta=I(T\le C)$. Kaplan-Meier estimates a time-to-event curve when censoring is conditionally independent. That assumption fails if risky accounts are watched longer; stratify, model censoring, or state bounds.

Intervention hides outcomes too. If a challenge stops an attacker, counterfactual loss is unknowable. “No incident after challenge” does not show every challenge was needed. Estimate effects from defensible comparisons and label prevented harm as an estimate with assumptions, never an observed count.

Defender and Attacker Learn at Different Speeds

Attackers infer thresholds by varying IP reputation, velocity, device age, and transaction amount. Tomorrow's distribution depends on today's policy:

$$P_{t+1}(X,Y)=\mathcal G(P_t,\pi_t,q_t),$$

with defender policy $\pi_t$ and attacker strategy $q_t$. Deployment changes the prediction population. Track tactic-conditioned performance, champion-shadow disagreement, score-boundary density, and audit yield below threshold. Aggregate stability can hide substitution from password spraying to session-cookie theft.

The answer is controlled exploration, rotating high-cost-to-mimic evidence, protected challenge sets, and versioned policy changes—not reflexive retraining. Canary cohorts help distinguish model change, sensor change, and adversary change.

Alerts Are Work, Risk Is Consequence

An alert reduction may mean better precision, missing telemetry, a raised threshold, attacker migration, deduplication, or detector failure. Northstar keeps four ledgers:

LedgerDenominatorMeasure
exposureprivileged identity-daysreachable value
adversaryaudited attemptshostile prevalence
interventioneligible decisionschallenge coverage
consequenceidentity-daysmature loss

Leadership gets a linked explanation, not one blended score. Exposure, prevalence, intervention, and mature consequence can move in opposite directions.

sequenceDiagram
    participant A as Attacker
    participant D as Detector
    participant U as User/Asset
    participant Q as Audit sampler
    participant L as Outcome ledger
    A->>D: adaptive attempt
    D->>U: allow/challenge/block
    D-->>A: observable friction
    Q->>U: probability-sampled review
    U-->>L: delayed report
    Q-->>L: weighted verdict
    L-->>D: mature evidence

The attacker receives fast feedback; the defender receives delayed, policy-mediated feedback.

Lab: Make a Good Dashboard Lie

This fixture simulates 200,000 logins. Attack prevalence rises, attackers suppress a visible feature, and the threshold rises to reduce queue volume. All alerts and one percent of non-alerts are reviewed. Save as ch09_adversarial_audit.py; run with the book .venv.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(19)
n = 200_000
era = np.repeat([0, 1], n // 2)
y = rng.binomial(1, np.where(era == 0, 0.0010, 0.0018))
velocity = rng.normal(0, 1, n) + y * np.where(era == 0, 3.2, 1.5)
device = rng.normal(0, 1, n) + y * 2.0
score = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-5.0 + 1.4 * velocity + device)))
alert = score >= np.where(era == 0, 0.18, 0.30)
audit = (~alert) & (rng.random(n) < 0.01)
reviewed = alert | audit
prob = np.where(alert, 1.0, 0.01)

for e, name in [(0, "before"), (1, "after")]:
    m = era == e
    weighted = np.sum(y[m & reviewed] / prob[m & reviewed]) / np.sum(m)
    print(f"{name}: alerts={alert[m].mean():.4%} incidents={y[m].mean():.4%} "
          f"precision={y[m & alert].mean():.2%} "
          f"reviewed_rate={y[m & reviewed].mean():.2%} "
          f"audit_weighted={weighted:.4%}")

Fixture: seed 19, 100,000 attempts per era, base rates 0.10/0.18 percent, audit probability 0.01, thresholds 0.18/0.30. Exact expected output:

before: alerts=2.2550% incidents=0.0960% precision=4.26% reviewed_rate=2.98% audit_weighted=0.0960%
after: alerts=0.8630% incidents=0.2010% precision=10.20% reviewed_rate=4.83% audit_weighted=0.1880%

Alerts fall 56 percent while incidents nearly double. Precision improves—excellent dashboard news. The weighted audit recovers the direction and approximate magnitude of harm. Sampling variance explains the difference from simulated truth. Production extensions should add score strata, label times, nonresponse, and confidence intervals. Inclusion probability is the indispensable field.

Reading the Failure as a System

The simulation reveals three distinct estimands. Alert precision asks what fraction of selected alerts are malicious. Reviewed-case rate mixes certain inclusion above threshold with rare inclusion below it and has no stable population meaning. Audit-weighted prevalence asks what fraction of all attempts are malicious, under the audit design. None alone estimates prevented loss.

A production review should reconstruct policy versions point in time. Re-score the same eligible attempts under old and new thresholds, then compare disagreement sets. Slice by attack mechanism rather than only user demographics: credential stuffing, adversary-in-the-middle, token theft, recovery abuse. Examine sensor reach and feature missingness within each slice. If one policy challenges and another allows a case, deliberately oversample that disagreement for audit while retaining a probability sample of the complete population.

Outcome delay needs a cohort ledger. For every weekly cohort publish attempts, known malicious, known benign, unresolved, censored, and estimated completion fraction. Do not backfill a historical dashboard silently; revisions are evidence. A mature cohort may still be biased if users who suffer small losses never report them, so combine customer reports, analyst investigation, recovery signals, and sampled inquiry without pretending they are interchangeable.

Capacity is part of policy. A threshold selected on an unconstrained curve may flood analysts, lengthen decision time, and lower effective recall. Evaluate at the queue's service rate. If arrivals exceed service, calculate the induced waiting-time distribution and the fraction of decisions made after the attacker completes the action. The model's offline recall is irrelevant to alerts nobody reaches.

Finally, distinguish calibration drift from ranking drift. A ranking may still order cases usefully while probabilities become wrong as prevalence changes. Recalibration can repair the former; tactic-specific ranking collapse needs new evidence or model structure. The random audit supports both diagnoses because it observes below-threshold outcomes.

Designing the Audit as a Durable Control

An audit stream fails if treated as a temporary research sample. It needs a budget owner, sampling service, case-handling service level, and exclusion review. The sampler commits the unit and inclusion probability before anyone sees the verdict; otherwise investigators can replace inconvenient selections and invalidate the design. Store frame size, stratum, draw identifier, selection time, contact attempts, disposition, and adjudication version.

A prevalence audit samples the eligible population. A threshold audit concentrates near a proposed boundary. An adaptation audit oversamples new tactics while preserving a probability component. Mixing objectives without separate weights creates an opaque convenience sample. Northstar runs named streams and prevents duplicate investigation from being counted twice.

Adjudication should be blind to production action when feasible. Knowing that a detector blocked a login can bias the verdict. Use a fixed evidence packet, dual review for ambiguous cases, and a reason taxonomy. Measure disagreement and resolution delay. A label is a versioned conclusion supported by evidence, not an eternal property revealed by one click.

Random audit creates operational risk: customer contact can disclose monitoring and investigations consume specialists. Use minimization, tiered collection, and stopping rules. At prevalence $p$ and sample size $n$, the chance of no positives is $(1-p)^n$. With $p=0.001$ and $n=1,000$, zero occurs about 36.8 percent of the time. A zero-yield week is unsurprising, not proof of safety.

The audit dashboard should emphasize design integrity: frame coverage, realized inclusion by stratum, unresolved rate, time to verdict, selection deviations, and weighted effective sample size. If these fail, suppress population estimates. “Not estimable this week” is better than a precise biased number. Publish the recovery condition as well, so operators know exactly which evidence defect must clear before estimates resume.

Analysts should also precompute escalation rules for surprising audit discoveries. One confirmed severe compromise in a supposedly protected stratum may justify immediate containment even though it cannot establish prevalence. Operational response and statistical inference have different thresholds: responders may act on credible individual evidence while analysts refuse to generalize from one case. The decision record should say which threshold fired.

Audit results must remain comparable across policy versions. Freeze stratum definitions for a reporting window or publish a bridge that reweights both versions to a common frame. If product growth changes the identity mix, standardize to a declared reference population and also show the unstandardized operational rate. This separates composition change from within-stratum risk.

Finally, protect the sampler from the detector team. If the same owners can modify thresholds, sampling probabilities, exclusions, and adjudication, favorable results become too easy to manufacture accidentally. Independent review of draw logs and exclusions is inexpensive assurance. The purpose is not distrust of individuals; it is maintaining an evidence channel that production optimization cannot silently erase.

Interview Practicum

You inherit Northstar's detector. Alerts fell 40 percent, confirmed incidents rose, and the VP asks whether the model failed. The interviewer releases facts only after useful questions: analysts review every alert but almost no allowed login; losses mature over 30 days; the newest cohort is seven days old; SOC raised the threshold six weeks ago; cookie theft grew after MFA rollout; a one-percent retrospective audit is permitted, but dangerous traffic may not be intentionally allowed.

A strong candidate refuses the binary diagnosis. They draw the observation process and separate event, decision, verdict, and loss times. They name threshold change, maturity, telemetry, attack mix, exposure, and queue behavior as competing mechanisms. They reconstruct mature cohorts under both policies and launch stratified audits below both thresholds. Primary outcomes are compromise or loss per identity-time; alerts and analyst minutes are constraints.

Halfway through, audit budget is cut in half. Preserve nonzero coverage in consequential strata, oversample policy disagreements and high-impact identities, record probabilities, and widen intervals. Then the interviewer reveals challenged attacks have no observable counterfactual loss. Stop calling them prevented incidents; use defensible comparisons and bounds.

The decision-quality close is: “I will neither roll back because incidents rose nor defend the model because precision improved. Freeze further threshold changes, restore point-in-time evaluation, start protected auditing, and decide after a mature cohort and audit interval exist.”

Worked Answer

In 48 hours, verify sensor counts, schema and deduplication changes, threshold history, queue limits, and incident definition. Rebuild cohorts by event time, attaching only evidence available at each historical decision. Show unresolved fractions and exclude immature cases from binary comparison.

Replay both thresholds on identical attempts. Report disagreements by tactic, identity value, device state, and coverage. Estimate population prevalence using the documented audit design. Keep analyst verdict and customer loss as distinct evidence. Compare expected loss at real review capacity, including challenge friction, false blocks, analyst cost, and mature harm. Require acceptable critical-stratum bounds and monitor below-threshold audit yield as an adaptation signal.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

Audited cases remain unresolved—now what? Treat response as a second selection process, report it by stratum, fix operational causes, and provide sensitivity bounds. Unresolved never becomes benign by timeout.

Can active learning replace random audit? No. It selects informative boundary cases and cannot estimate population prevalence without a known design. Run both streams for different purposes.

How can adaptation be seen before labels mature? Monitor tactic mix, boundary density, missingness, challenge abandonment, canary traffic, and shadow disagreement. They are warnings, not outcome proof.

When should a feature be retired? When cheap manipulation, brittle group effects, or tactic-conditioned replay show lost incremental value. Retire through a shadowed policy change.

Key Takeaways

  • Production labels describe a policy-selected sample, not the threat population.
  • Rare-event arithmetic makes accuracy and raw alert counts misleading.
  • Preserve event time, availability time, maturity, censoring, and sampling probability.
  • A protected probability audit reveals risk below the threshold; active learning does not replace it.
  • Attackers receive faster feedback than defenders, demanding tactic- and cohort-aware evaluation.
  • Alert volume measures work; mature consequence and uncertainty measure security.
