Chapter 18: Ground Truth Is a Process, Not a Column

A phishing verdict does not arrive from nature. It is assembled from an artifact, evidence visible at review time, an analyst’s policy interpretation, later incident facts, and sometimes remediation behavior. Two competent analysts can disagree because “malicious” conflates distinct questions: Was the sender deceptive? Did the message carry a harmful capability? Would it work in this environment? Was the sender account compromised? Did the recipient act?

Treating a ticket’s final value as immutable truth hides these distinctions and trains models on workflow artifacts. Ground truth should be represented as versioned claims with provenance, uncertainty, and a label policy. The goal is not zero disagreement. It is to know which disagreement is error, which reveals an underspecified taxonomy, and which expresses irreducible ambiguity.

Define Claims Before Labels

A useful phishing ontology separates observable claims:

  • sender_control: legitimate, spoofed, compromised, unknown;
  • intent: benign, credential theft, malware delivery, fraud, unknown;
  • capability: inert, live link, executable, exploit, unknown;
  • recipient_exposure: delivered, quarantined, opened, clicked, credentials entered;
  • case_disposition: benign, policy violation, attempted attack, confirmed incident;
  • confidence: a probability or behaviorally anchored ordered scale.

A binary target can be derived for a particular decision. A quarantine model might define positive as credible malicious intent plus active capability. An incident model requires compromise evidence. Reusing one malicious column for both creates semantic error.

The guide must define an evidence cutoff. If historical labels include a compromise discovered weeks later, evaluation at delivery time contains hindsight. Record what was knowable, not simply what became known.

Store Assertions, Not Overwrites

CREATE TABLE label_assertion (
    assertion_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY,
    object_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    task_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    taxonomy_version TEXT NOT NULL,
    reviewer_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    reviewer_role TEXT NOT NULL,
    label_value TEXT NOT NULL,
    confidence REAL CHECK (confidence BETWEEN 0 AND 1),
    evidence_cutoff TEXT NOT NULL,
    created_at TEXT NOT NULL,
    supersedes_id TEXT,
    rationale_code TEXT NOT NULL,
    FOREIGN KEY (supersedes_id) REFERENCES label_assertion(assertion_id)
);

CREATE TABLE evidence_item (
    evidence_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY,
    object_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    observed_at TEXT NOT NULL,
    evidence_type TEXT NOT NULL,
    source_system TEXT NOT NULL,
    payload_digest TEXT NOT NULL,
    available_at TEXT NOT NULL
);

Never update a verdict in place. Append an assertion that supersedes it. This permits point-in-time reconstruction and separates correction from a taxonomy change. Store evidence by digest and controlled reference rather than duplicating dangerous attachments into the labeling database.

Agreement Is a Diagnostic

Raw agreement is inflated by a dominant class. For two reviewers, Cohen’s kappa is

\[ \kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e}, \]

where \(p_o\) is observed agreement and \(p_e\) is expected agreement from reviewer marginals. With rare positives, kappa may be low despite high raw agreement. Report the confusion table and positive- and negative-class agreement as well.

For multiple reviewers, missing assignments, or ordinal outcomes, Krippendorff’s alpha is more flexible:

\[ \alpha=1-\frac{D_o}{D_e}. \]

Its disagreement distance must match the scale: nominal labels use equality, while ordered confidence should penalize distant ratings more.

Agreement is not correctness. Analysts can agree perfectly on a bad policy. Slice disagreement by campaign, language, attachment, evidence availability, queue age, and reviewer pair. Disputes concentrated around compromised legitimate senders indicate a taxonomy gap rather than careless work.

Adjudication Develops Policy

Do not send every conflict to a senior analyst and declare the result gold. Record why it occurred:

  1. clerical error;
  2. missed evidence;
  3. ambiguous artifact;
  4. taxonomy gap;
  5. policy conflict;
  6. evidence unavailable at initial review.

Only the first two imply simple correction. Taxonomy gaps require a guide revision and analysis of which historical labels change. Preserve pre-adjudication votes; otherwise agreement looks artificially high and reviewer error models cannot be estimated.

Gold sets need expiration. Maintain a stable anchor subset for longitudinal comparison and a rotating challenge subset for current techniques. Keep them out of training, restrict access, and use equivalent variants so reviewers and models cannot memorize them.

Positive–Unlabeled Learning

Confirmed attacks may be reliable positives, while “negative” means “not investigated.” Let \(s=1\) mean labeled positive and \(y=1\) truly positive. Under selected-completely-at-random labeling among positives,

\[ c=P(s=1\mid y=1), \qquad P(y=1\mid x)=\frac{P(s=1\mid x)}{c}. \]

Elkan and Noto estimate \(c\) using held-out known positives. The assumption is demanding. Obvious and severe attacks are more likely to be reviewed, so selection depends on features. PU learning is useful as sensitivity analysis, not absolution. Document selection and review a probability sample of unlabeled items. Calling all unreviewed events negative is usually worse, but the correction is only credible to the extent its selection assumptions are.

Weak Supervision

Labeling functions might encode known credential domains, DMARC failure with display-name impersonation, sandbox detonation, or an analyst escalation. Snorkel’s key contribution is to model differing accuracy and dependence rather than use majority vote.

Security rules are correlated. Blocklists may share an upstream feed; several attachment rules may all detect macros. Assuming independence yields overconfident labels. Group rules by provenance, encode known dependencies, and validate probabilistic labels on an expert set. A labeling function should abstain when its precondition is absent instead of emitting a negative.

Probabilistic labels preserve uncertainty during training, but the deployed decision still needs action-specific costs and thresholds. A 0.6 probability may justify sandboxing but not disabling an account.

Safe Lab: Reliability-Weighted Disagreement

This synthetic lab computes kappa and combines two conflicting votes using known sensitivity, specificity, and prevalence. Conditional independence is an assumption to test, not a fact.

items = {
    "m1": {"a": 1, "b": 1},
    "m2": {"a": 1, "b": 0},
    "m3": {"a": 0, "b": 0},
    "m4": {"a": 0, "b": 0},
    "m5": {"a": 1, "b": 1},
    "m6": {"a": 0, "b": 1},
}

def cohen_kappa(rows, left="a", right="b"):
    pairs = [(v[left], v[right]) for v in rows.values()]
    po = sum(x == y for x, y in pairs) / len(pairs)
    pa = sum(x for x, _ in pairs) / len(pairs)
    pb = sum(y for _, y in pairs) / len(pairs)
    pe = pa * pb + (1 - pa) * (1 - pb)
    return (po - pe) / (1 - pe)

def posterior_positive(votes, sensitivity, specificity, prevalence):
    pos, neg = prevalence, 1 - prevalence
    for reviewer, vote in votes.items():
        pos *= sensitivity[reviewer] if vote else 1 - sensitivity[reviewer]
        neg *= 1 - specificity[reviewer] if vote else specificity[reviewer]
    return pos / (pos + neg)

kappa = cohen_kappa(items)
assert round(kappa, 3) == 0.333
p = posterior_positive(
    {"a": 1, "b": 0},
    sensitivity={"a": 0.90, "b": 0.75},
    specificity={"a": 0.80, "b": 0.95},
    prevalence=0.10,
)
assert round(p, 3) == 0.116
print(f"kappa={kappa:.3f}, disputed_p={p:.3f}")

Expected output:

kappa=0.333, disputed_p=0.116

The disputed object is not automatically positive because reviewer A voted positive. Low prevalence and B’s high specificity matter. The posterior is a prioritization aid, not adjudicated fact; its estimated reviewer rates and independence assumption must travel with it.

Sampling and Active Learning

Random review estimates population error but spends expertise on easy negatives. Uncertainty sampling improves boundaries but can repeatedly select outliers. A defensible batch combines:

  • a probability sample for prevalence and quality estimates;
  • uncertain cases for model improvement;
  • disagreement cases for policy development;
  • high-impact cases for safety;
  • new temporal and campaign strata for drift.

Record inclusion probability. Metrics from an actively selected batch are biased unless weighted or evaluated on a separate probability sample. Attackers can manufacture uncertain artifacts to exhaust review capacity, so cap contributions by campaign, tenant, sender, or infrastructure cluster.

Delayed and Censored Outcomes

A message labeled benign today may be connected to an incident later. Model the state transition rather than overwrite history: provisional_benign -> confirmed_attack at a recorded time. Evaluation at 14 days should treat unresolved recent items as censored, not negative. Report label maturation curves: the fraction of provisional outcomes that change after 7, 30, and 90 days.

Remediation is evidence but not ground truth. A user resetting credentials may reflect caution rather than compromise; failure to remediate may reflect ownership confusion. Keep action, analyst conclusion, and incident outcome as separate assertions.

Estimating Reviewer Behavior Without Ranking People

The Dawid–Skene model gives a more principled alternative to selecting the most senior vote. For latent class \(z_i\) and reviewer confusion matrix \(\pi^{(r)}{kl}=P(y{ir}=l\mid z_i=k)\), expectation–maximization alternates between estimating each item’s class posterior and updating reviewer matrices from those soft assignments. The result can identify a reviewer who is highly specific but misses subtle attacks, or one who is sensitive but overcalls benign simulations.

Identifiability is not guaranteed. If all reviewers copy the same upstream tool, their votes do not provide independent evidence. If reviewers see disjoint queues, differences mix reviewer behavior with case mix. Include overlapping assignments, randomize presentation order, conceal other votes, and ensure enough positive anchors. Regularize estimates for reviewers with few cases; a raw 100 percent score from three examples is not credible.

Use reviewer models to improve routing and training, not to produce a simplistic employee leaderboard. Analysts may specialize legitimately. Share error examples and policy changes, give reviewers a correction mechanism, and evaluate whether the evidence interface caused mistakes. A reviewer confusion matrix is conditional on task, taxonomy, evidence, and time.

Label quality also has a cost frontier. Suppose an item receives one initial review, a second review when confidence is low, and panel adjudication only when the action would be consequential. Estimate marginal corrected errors per expert hour at each stage. More votes are not always better: correlated reviewers can add confidence without information. Allocate additional review where expected decision regret—the probability the label is wrong multiplied by the consequence of using it—is greatest.

When taxonomy versions change, create a migration map rather than silently translating old labels. Some categories split, some merge, and some become non-comparable. Re-label a stratified bridge sample under both versions to estimate discontinuity. Dashboards should mark the boundary; a sudden fall in “malicious” after separating policy violations from attacks is a semantic change, not an improvement.

Label-Lineage Tests

Treat label production like a data pipeline. Unit-test allowed taxonomy values and transition rules. Assert that every derived training label references a policy version and evidence cutoff; that no evidence timestamp exceeds the decision timestamp; that supersession chains are acyclic; and that objects in the final test period were absent from weak-supervision rule development. A daily reconciliation should flag assertions whose referenced evidence was deleted or whose reviewer account was not authorized for the task.

Statistical tests should detect process shifts: reviewer positive rate by queue, disagreement by artifact family, time-to-label, abstention rate, and the fraction resolved through adjudication. A shift is not automatically bad—an attack campaign can legitimately change prevalence—but it triggers investigation before retraining. Publish a label-data sheet describing intended uses, exclusions, selection, maturity, known blind spots, and the difference between analyst judgment and confirmed outcome. Models inherit these limitations even when their feature engineering is flawless. Label debt compounds quietly until production decisions expose it.

Production Failure Analysis

Closed loop. The model determines review; reviewed alerts become training labels; suppressed attacks remain unknown. Maintain exploration sampling.

Hindsight leakage. Later incident evidence enters an earlier target or feature. Enforce evidence cutoffs and temporal joins.

Reviewer drift. Policy changes without taxonomy versioning. Re-score anchors and estimate behavior by reviewer and version.

Adjudicator dominance. One senior opinion becomes truth. Preserve dissent and compare adjudication with later outcomes.

Queue bias. Campaign pressure changes review quality. Store workload, queue age, and duration as process metadata, never as predictive evidence of maliciousness.

Benchmark contamination. Memorable gold examples leak into development. Restrict, rotate, and hash-check where feasible.

Semantic collapse. One flag combines intent, capability, exposure, and impact. Preserve components and derive labels per decision.

Identity leakage. Reviewer identity or queue route becomes a model feature and predicts institutional behavior. Exclude it from artifact models while retaining it for process audits.

  • Elkan and Noto, “Learning Classifiers from Only Positive and Unlabeled Data.” The selected-positive formulation is useful precisely because it makes review-selection assumptions visible. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1553374.1553453>
  • Ratner et al., “Snorkel: Rapid Training Data Creation with Weak Supervision.” The work shows how to combine noisy, overlapping sources rather than canonize rule votes. <https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p269-ratner.pdf>

Interview Practicum

Scenario: Thirty-Five Percent Disagreement.

Prompt

Two analyst teams disagree on 35 percent of sampled phishing messages. Leadership wants one ground-truth column by Friday so a classifier can launch.

Clarifying Questions

Ask whether disagreement concerns intent, successful compromise, or quarantine action; how items were sampled; whether both teams saw identical evidence; whether teams specialize by queue; and what action the classifier automates. Determine whether 35 percent is overall or positive-class disagreement.

Worked Answer

Do not manufacture consensus. Preserve assertions, build a cross-tab, and run a blinded overlap study on a probability sample with identical evidence packets. Calculate class-specific agreement and kappa or alpha. Classify conflict reasons and split intent, capability, exposure, and outcome where the taxonomy collapses them. Revise the guide, adjudicate against it, relabel an impact sample, and retain a separate temporal test set.

Use probabilistic labels where ambiguity is real, but define hard actions from operational costs. Monitor reviewer confusion matrices and later incident evidence; seniority and majority vote are not truth mechanisms.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

Constraint Change

The panel reveals Team A reviews user reports and Team B reviews gateway alerts; the disagreement set was made by joining queues.

The statistic is not interpretable because assignment depends on source and severity. Sample from the underlying delivered-email population and randomly send identical packets to both teams. Estimate queue selection and report it. Do not rank team accuracy until case mix is controlled. The routing policy may be the primary source of disagreement.

Weak Answers and Follow-Ups

Majority vote fails with two correlated teams. Dropping disputes creates an easy dataset. Sending all disputes to a lead hides policy ambiguity. Soft labels alone do not fix selection bias.

If outcomes arrive 60 days later, preserve provisional point-in-time labels and measure their transition to delayed evidence; unresolved objects are censored. If budget is halved, preserve probability sampling, then divide the remainder among uncertainty, disagreement, impact, and novelty. In thirty seconds: compare like cases with like evidence, version every assertion, repair semantics, and let delayed outcomes calibrate the process rather than erase its history.

Key Takeaways

  • A label is a claim under an evidence cutoff and policy, not an intrinsic column.
  • Separate intent, capability, exposure, and outcome before deriving task targets.
  • Agreement measures reliability, not correctness.
  • PU and weak-supervision methods depend on explicit selection and dependence assumptions.
  • Preserve probability sampling; active-learning batches cannot estimate population quality by themselves.
  • Version assertions and retain disagreement because provenance is part of ground truth.
