Chapter 48: Case Study: Evidence-Driven Security Program

An enterprise security program is usually described as a list of tools, controls, incidents, and compliance percentages. Those objects do not compose into evidence. A green control dashboard can coexist with an expanding attack path; fewer incidents can reflect prevention, attacker quiet, or broken sensors; a risk score can conceal assumptions that no executive agreed to.

This final capstone designs an evidence system for Meridian, a global software and payments company. It integrates the book's semantic contracts, telemetry engineering, threat models, detection measurement, vulnerability prioritization, causal evaluation, AI assurance, production reliability, team design, and decision communication. The goal is not a universal cyber number. It is a traceable chain from business assets and plausible threats through control claims and observations to decisions, uncertainty, and accountable work.

Semantic architecture: facts before dashboards

Meridian defines a small enterprise security ontology. Core entities are BusinessService, Asset, Identity, DataClass, ThreatScenario, AttackPath, Control, ControlClaim, Evidence, Observation, Finding, Incident, Decision, and Owner. Every metric declares its population, event time, knowledge time, grain, exclusions, source health, and version.

Canonical facts are append-only. Corrections supersede prior records; they do not silently rewrite history. Operational systems retain local schemas, while semantic adapters map them into governed facts. This avoids forcing endpoint, cloud, identity, and application telemetry into one lossy mega-table.

A metric definition resembles an API contract. For example, identity_compromise_harm_30d specifies an episode population, verified harmful outcomes within 30 days, label maturity, intervention status, and excluded test tenants. Its dashboard cannot substitute raw alert count without changing the metric version.

Data quality is risk-weighted. Completeness for production control-plane logs receives a tighter SLO than completeness for low-risk laboratory endpoints. Health dimensions include expected volume, entity join rate, latency, schema conformity, clock skew, duplication, and outcome linkage. A sensor-health incident annotates dependent evidence so downstream reports do not treat absence as safety.

Control claims and evidence graph

“MFA is deployed” is an implementation fact, not a security claim. Meridian expresses a control as falsifiable propositions:

control_claim(
 claim_id, control_id, subject_scope, threat_edge,
 proposition, target, valid_from, valid_to,
 claim_owner, review_frequency, status
)

evidence_assertion(
 assertion_id, claim_id, evidence_type, artifact_uri,
 observed_at, population_start, population_end,
 result, uncertainty_low, uncertainty_high,
 freshness_deadline, producer, method_version
)

dependency(
 upstream_claim_id, downstream_claim_id,
 dependency_type, rationale
)

An identity claim might state: “For workforce administrator access to production, phishing-resistant authentication interrupts credential replay in at least 99% of eligible attempts, with telemetry coverage above 99.5%.” Evidence can include configuration attestations, sampled access logs, replay tests, red-team results, exceptions, and outage history. Each item has scope and freshness. A policy document alone cannot satisfy an effectiveness claim.

The graph separates four evidence classes:

  • Design evidence: architecture, policy, threat analysis, and test specification.
  • Implementation evidence: configuration, deployment, inventory, and attestation.
  • Operating evidence: logs, sampling, SLOs, exceptions, and failure recovery.
  • Outcome evidence: attack interruption, incident impact, remediation, and causal estimates.

Claim confidence is not a naive average. Suppose implementation depends on sensor coverage and policy enforcement. The confidence in the conjunction cannot exceed its weakest required dependency. Correlated evidence from the same source receives less weight than independent evidence. One usable conservative aggregation is

\[ C(claim)=\min_{d\in required}C(d)\times Freshness\times ScopeCoverage, \]

with uncertainty retained, not rounded into traffic-light theater. The graph permits queries such as “Which high-loss attack paths depend on a stale identity-provider assertion?” and “Which executive claims rely on the same failing asset inventory?”

Threat portfolio and control coverage

Meridian manages threat scenarios rather than an undifferentiated risk register. A scenario states actor, objective, entry condition, attack path, affected services, loss mechanisms, current controls, observability, and plausible frequency/severity ranges. Examples include cloud-control-plane takeover, software supply-chain compromise, payment credential theft, destructive ransomware, and AI-agent misuse.

Attack paths are directed graphs. A control maps to an edge only when a falsifiable claim explains how it prevents, detects, delays, or recovers that transition. Coverage has dimensions: population scope, time, implementation, demonstrated effectiveness, and independence. Five tools observing one edge do not imply fivefold protection.

Scenario loss is a distribution:

\[ L_s=I_s\times D_s+R_s+C_s, \]

where \(I_s\) is business interruption per unit time, \(D_s\) duration, \(R_s\) response/recovery cost, and \(C_s\) customer, legal, or contractual consequence. Frequency and loss ranges come from internal episodes, near misses, external evidence, expert elicitation, and stress tests. Monte Carlo simulation can propagate uncertainty, but the result remains conditional on the scenario model.

Prioritization uses marginal risk reduction per constrained unit, adjusted for strategic dependencies:

\[ Value(j)=\frac{E[L\mid current]-E[L\mid control_j]}{Cost_j}\times Enablement_j. \]

This does not reduce the portfolio to a greedy ratio. Some investments—asset identity, telemetry lineage, strong authentication, safe deployment—enable many later controls. The roadmap therefore balances immediate loss reduction, foundational leverage, regulatory obligations, and concentration risk.

Detection, incidents, and vulnerabilities in one portfolio

Detection metrics connect to scenario edges. Report eligible attack opportunities, sensor coverage, detection probability with intervals, time from first observable event to decision, analyst capacity, containment time, and missed/late evidence. Alert volume is a workload measure.

Incident outcomes use severity-adjusted business harm, time to containment and recovery, recurrence, and control-learning closure. Incident counts are shown with exposure and sensor health. A postmortem creates or updates graph evidence; it does not close until systemic actions have owners and validation criteria.

Vulnerabilities are clustered into remediation units and joined to deployment exposure, business service, exploit evidence, and compensating controls. The queue optimizes avoidable loss under engineering capacity, while mandatory policy lanes remain visible. Survival and recurrence reveal whether teams retire root causes or merely close tickets.

Safe runnable SQL lab: find unsupported executive claims

This SQLite lab builds a miniature claims/evidence graph and queries stale or insufficient assertions. It uses no production data.

CREATE TABLE claims(
 claim_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY, scenario TEXT, proposition TEXT,
 min_coverage REAL, max_age_days INTEGER, executive_visible INTEGER
);
CREATE TABLE evidence(
 evidence_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY, claim_id TEXT, observed_date TEXT,
 coverage REAL, result TEXT, independent_source TEXT
);

INSERT INTO claims VALUES
('C1','cloud takeover','Admin MFA blocks replay',0.995,30,1),
('C2','supply chain','Release artifacts are provenance-verified',0.990,14,1),
('C3','ransomware','Tier-0 recovery meets 8-hour objective',0.950,90,1);
INSERT INTO evidence VALUES
('E1','C1','2026-07-28',0.998,'pass','idp-log'),
('E2','C1','2026-07-29',0.997,'pass','red-team'),
('E3','C2','2026-06-01',0.999,'pass','ci-config'),
('E4','C3','2026-07-15',0.910,'pass','recovery-test');

WITH latest AS (
 SELECT c.claim_id, c.scenario, c.proposition, c.min_coverage, c.max_age_days,
        MAX(e.observed_date) AS last_seen,
        MIN(e.coverage) AS demonstrated_coverage,
        COUNT(DISTINCT e.independent_source) AS independent_sources
 FROM claims c LEFT JOIN evidence e USING(claim_id)
 WHERE c.executive_visible=1 GROUP BY c.claim_id
)
SELECT claim_id, scenario,
 CASE
  WHEN last_seen IS NULL THEN 'NO_EVIDENCE'
  WHEN julianday('2026-08-20')-julianday(last_seen) > max_age_days THEN 'STALE'
  WHEN demonstrated_coverage < min_coverage THEN 'INSUFFICIENT_SCOPE'
  WHEN independent_sources < 2 THEN 'SINGLE_SOURCE'
  ELSE 'SUPPORTED'
 END AS status
FROM latest ORDER BY claim_id;

Expected output:

C1|cloud takeover|SUPPORTED
C2|supply chain|STALE
C3|ransomware|INSUFFICIENT_SCOPE

The query is intentionally conservative: it uses minimum demonstrated coverage and requires two independent sources. In production, evidence applicability is interval- and population-specific, and dependence is more nuanced than counting source names. Still, this simple test catches a common governance failure: a current dashboard backed by stale evidence.

Executive reporting as decision support

The quarterly executive report begins with decisions, not telemetry. A one-page portfolio view contains:

  1. material scenario movement and why;
  2. controls whose evidence strengthened, weakened, or expired;
  3. realized incidents and near misses linked to scenarios;
  4. leading exposure changes such as internet-facing privilege or unsupported software;
  5. investments requiring acceptance, acceleration, or stop decisions;
  6. uncertainty and data-health exceptions.

Every statement is tagged as fact, estimate, assumption, or recommendation. Ranges replace false precision. The report may say: “Cloud takeover exposure likely decreased after phishing-resistant administrator authentication; implementation evidence covers 99.8% of eligible access and red-team replay was blocked, but two break-glass paths lack independent testing.” That is more actionable than “identity risk: 72/100.”

Board reporting uses fewer scenarios and emphasizes loss mechanisms, resilience, concentration, trend evidence, and management decisions. Operational drill-down remains available through the graph. Compliance mappings are generated from claims and evidence but do not become the enterprise risk model: passing a control test and reducing a material threat are related, not identical.

Operating model and measurement governance

A small central security data-science and evidence team owns ontology, causal standards, shared platforms, and portfolio synthesis. Embedded analysts own domain features and decisions in identity, cloud, product security, detection, and resilience. Security engineering owns control operation; business-service owners own risk decisions; internal audit independently tests selected claims.

Every production metric has a named owner, semantic version, review cadence, source SLO, and retirement condition. Every model has point-in-time reproducibility, calibration monitoring, rollback, and an intervention policy. Every experiment has an estimand and ethical boundary. The decision registry records the evidence available, alternatives considered, expected outcome, owner, review date, and what would reverse the decision.

The program avoids performance traps. Analysts are not rewarded for alert counts, red teams for raw findings, or engineering teams for ticket closure alone. Shared outcomes include demonstrated attack-path interruption, durable remediation, reduced recovery time, evidence freshness, and decision turnaround—balanced by user friction, privacy, cost, and reliability.

Twelve-month roadmap

Quarter 1: establish truth and choose the portfolio

  • Approve the ontology, metric contract template, evidence classes, and decision registry.
  • Identify the top five threat scenarios with business owners and loss ranges.
  • Inventory telemetry dependencies and define risk-weighted source SLOs.
  • Build canonical identity, asset, service, and control identifiers; quantify unresolved joins.
  • Baseline current incident harm, detection coverage, vulnerability survival, and control-evidence freshness.

Exit criteria: scenario owners agree on attack paths; 80% of Tier-0 services map to accountable assets and identities; executive claims expose source and freshness; known data gaps have funded owners.

Quarter 2: prove two vertical slices

  • Deliver an identity-compromise slice from event contract through intervention and friction measurement.
  • Deliver a software-supply-chain slice from build provenance through release control claims and recovery drill.
  • Launch randomized label audits and independent evidence sampling.
  • Connect vulnerability remediation units to services and deployment exposure.
  • Train domain teams to write falsifiable claims and uncertainty-aware decision memos.

Exit criteria: both slices support point-in-time replay, have tested failure modes, and produce a decision that changes resource allocation or policy.

Quarter 3: evaluate controls and scale operations

  • Run causal or quasi-experimental evaluations for two high-cost controls.
  • Deploy detection-capacity optimization and remediation survival reporting.
  • Automate claim freshness, scope, and dependency checks.
  • Conduct cloud takeover, ransomware recovery, and coding-agent containment drills.
  • Establish model release gates, calibration review, and rollback across security analytics.

Exit criteria: at least two controls have credible effect and friction estimates; stale executive claims automatically downgrade; drill actions are validated rather than merely assigned.

Quarter 4: institutionalize portfolio decisions

  • Produce the first evidence-linked annual threat portfolio and budget options.
  • Quantify risk reduction ranges and foundational enablement from completed investments.
  • Retire vanity metrics and duplicate dashboards through metric-contract governance.
  • Add audit sampling and board drill-down to the evidence graph.
  • Review team topology, skills, privacy controls, retention, and next-year scenario changes.

Exit criteria: the budget memo traces proposed spending to scenarios, claims, evidence gaps, expected outcomes, and owners; executives can inspect uncertainty; next-year priorities follow measured residual exposure rather than tool renewal dates.

Failure drills

Executive metric contradiction. Incident reporting says ransomware recovery improved while recovery exercises show Tier-0 regression. Trace definitions, populations, and censoring; suspend the aggregate claim; publish the contradiction and decision needed. Negative evidence is not a communications defect.

Semantic breaking change. A cloud provider changes principal identifiers, collapsing join coverage. Quarantine dependent metrics, version the adapter, replay the affected period, and annotate every evidence assertion generated during degradation.

Control monoculture. Three “independent” claims rely on one identity provider. Simulate provider compromise or outage, update dependency edges, and fund a recovery or alternate-verification path. Tool count is not defense independence.

Roadmap capture. A loud stakeholder requests an AI dashboard unrelated to top scenarios. Require a decision, user, evidence gap, and expected portfolio effect. Prototype only if it competes successfully against committed work; novelty is not priority.

Sources and Further Reading

  • NIST, Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, supplies Govern/Identify/Protect/Detect/Respond/Recover outcomes. The evidence graph makes those outcomes falsifiable rather than treating framework categories as metrics.
  • NIST, Risk Management Framework SP 800-37 Rev. 2, informs lifecycle authorization, continuous monitoring, and accountable risk decisions.
  • NIST, Security and Privacy Controls SP 800-53 Rev. 5, provides a control catalog; this chapter distinguishes catalog mapping, implementation evidence, operating evidence, and outcome evidence.
  • NIST, Guide for Conducting Risk Assessments SP 800-30 Rev. 1, supports threat, vulnerability, likelihood, and impact reasoning while preserving uncertainty.
  • MITRE, ATT&CK, supports attack-path and detection mappings. Technique coverage does not by itself prove prevention or detection effectiveness.
  • Open Group, Open FAIR Standard, offers a quantitative loss-event vocabulary useful for scenario ranges; estimates remain conditional on evidence and assumptions.
  • NIST, AI RMF 1.0, extends governance to AI-enabled controls and products, including validity, transparency, monitoring, and accountability.

Interview Practicum

You join a company after a material cloud breach. The board wants one cyber-risk score in 30 days. Security owns 140 dashboards, audit reports 92% control compliance, asset inventory joins only 68% of cloud resources to a business service, and the CISO must submit next year's budget in eight weeks. Design the response without promising a mature quantitative program in a month.

Worked Answer

I would not manufacture a scalar. In 30 days I would produce a decision-grade provisional portfolio. First, reconstruct the breach as a scenario and attack path, then select four or five material scenarios with business owners: likely cloud takeover, supply chain, ransomware, payment-data theft, and a company-specific case. For each I would state assets, entry conditions, loss mechanisms, controls, evidence, gaps, and uncertainty.

The 68% join rate is not a footnote; it bounds every service-level claim. I would prioritize Tier-0 and externally exposed resources, measure unresolved populations, and create a temporary “unknown ownership” risk segment rather than dropping those assets. The 92% compliance figure would be decomposed into design, implementation, operation, and outcome evidence. I would test freshness and independence for the claims most relevant to the breach.

For the board, I would present scenario ranges and confidence, recent movement, concentration, control evidence that survived or failed, recovery readiness, and three decisions. If a visual summary is required, I would use ordinal evidence states with drill-down, not arithmetic that implies unsupported precision. I would explicitly label facts, estimates, assumptions, and recommendations.

For the eight-week budget, I would compare options by marginal scenario reduction, foundational enablement, obligations, cost, and time to evidence. Likely first investments are identity and asset semantics, control-plane telemetry, strong administrator authentication, tested recovery, and a claims/evidence layer—not another dashboard. Each funded item receives an owner, falsifiable claim, measurement plan, and stop/review date.

At Principal level, I would use the crisis to simplify governance: freeze creation of uncontracted metrics, register executive claims, establish source SLOs, select two end-to-end vertical slices, and define a 12-month roadmap. I would tell the board what can be known in 30 days, what cannot, and which instrumentation will reduce uncertainty. Credibility comes from traceability and bounded claims, not a polished number.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

What if the board insists on a number? Provide explicitly modeled scenario loss ranges and, if necessary, a portfolio distribution with assumptions and sensitivity—not an opaque 0–100 score. Pair it with the largest uncertainty drivers.

How do you choose the first vertical slice? Select a material scenario with an imminent decision, usable telemetry, an intervention owner, and measurable harm/friction. Avoid choosing only the easiest data.

Can compliance evidence populate the graph? Yes, with scope, time, method, and evidence class. A passed audit sample supports a bounded assertion; it does not automatically prove attack-path effectiveness.

How do you show program value after one year? Demonstrated control effects, retired exposure, faster containment/recovery, durable remediation, fresher independent evidence, and better decisions—alongside cost, friction, privacy, and remaining uncertainty.

Key Takeaways

  • Build enterprise security measurement on versioned semantic facts, not dashboard names.
  • Express controls as falsifiable, scoped claims connected to fresh and independent evidence.
  • Manage a threat-scenario portfolio with attack paths, loss ranges, dependencies, and residual uncertainty.
  • Executive reporting should expose decisions, evidence changes, assumptions, and contradictions.
  • A credible roadmap alternates foundational data work with end-to-end decisions that prove practical value.

Field lesson: platform evidence can mature before outcome evidence

The sec-pr assurance program established a canonical population of 1,287 PR iterations, 1,428 scan runs, 1,098 candidate findings, 566 evidence artifacts, and 564 benchmark contracts. It also exposed 278 unresolved records through quarantine and preserved unknown outcomes instead of fabricating labels. For program leaders, this is a useful maturity pattern: first make the evidence supply chain observable and governed; then acquire adjudicated outcomes; only then authorize effectiveness and investment claims.
