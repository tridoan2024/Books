Chapter 6: Visualization, Communication, and Analytical Storytelling

Redesign Studio: Four Charts Enter, One Decision Leaves

The wall display says alert volume is down 42 percent. The security vice president calls the program green. An analyst points out that confirmed compromises doubled. Both statements are true, and the dashboard has made them appear contradictory because it visualizes production rather than exposure, quality, or consequence.

This chapter is a critique studio. Each station begins with a chart that can be computed correctly and still mislead. We calculate the replacement, specify visual encodings, and state what the display permits a reader to conclude. There is no generic dashboard architecture here. The unit of work is a visual claim.

Station 1: Replace the Alert Mountain

Suppose two weeks have these counts:

week  eligible_logins  alerts  reviewed  confirmed_compromises
W1    2,000,000        1,000   900       20
W2    3,500,000          580   540       42

A bar chart of alerts celebrates a fall from 1,000 to 580. Its denominator is invisible, review coverage changed, and the outcome moved in the opposite direction. Calculate rates per 100,000 eligible logins and precision among reviewed alerts:

rows = [
    {"week":"W1","eligible":2_000_000,"alerts":1_000,"reviewed":900,"confirmed":20},
    {"week":"W2","eligible":3_500_000,"alerts":580,"reviewed":540,"confirmed":42},
]
for r in rows:
    r["alert_rate"] = 100_000*r["alerts"]/r["eligible"]
    r["compromise_rate"] = 100_000*r["confirmed"]/r["eligible"]
    r["review_precision"] = r["confirmed"]/r["reviewed"]
    print(r["week"], f'{r["alert_rate"]:.2f}', f'{r["compromise_rate"]:.2f}',
          f'{100*r["review_precision"]:.2f}%')

Exact output:

W1 50.00 1.00 2.22%
W2 16.57 1.20 7.78%

The redesign is a small multiple, not a dual-axis chart: alert rate and confirmed-compromise rate get separate aligned panels with the same weekly x-axis; review precision appears as a third panel. Each mark includes numerator and denominator in its tooltip or adjacent annotation. The decision memo now reads: alert burden per login fell sharply, reviewed-alert yield increased, yet observed compromise incidence rose 20 percent. That combination calls for a coverage investigation, not a green status.

Rare-event denominators

Never divide by “all traffic” when only a subset could generate the event. A privileged-action detector should use eligible privileged actions, not HTTP requests. A phishing report rate might use delivered messages or active mailboxes depending on the question; those denominators answer different questions. Display the population definition and observation window beside the chart.

When denominators are small, raw rates jump. For 1 compromise in 100 logins, the observed rate is 1 percent, but uncertainty is wide. Wilson's 95 percent interval for a binomial proportion is

\[ \frac{\hat p+z^2/(2n)\;\pm\;z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n+z^2/(4n^2)}}{1+z^2/n}. \]

The following code computes it without a statistics package:

from math import sqrt
def wilson(k: int, n: int, z: float=1.96) -> tuple[float,float]:
    p=k/n; d=1+z*z/n
    c=(p+z*z/(2*n))/d
    h=z*sqrt(p*(1-p)/n+z*z/(4*n*n))/d
    return c-h,c+h
print(tuple(round(100*x,2) for x in wilson(1,100)))

Expected output is (0.18, 5.45). Plot the point with this interval; do not give a tiny team the same visual certainty as a population of millions.

Station 2: Audit the Geometry

Three common choices create false drama or false calm.

Truncated bars. Bars encode length from a baseline. Showing values 96 and 100 on an axis beginning at 95 makes a four-percent change occupy eighty percent of the drawing. Begin ordinary bars at zero. If the meaningful question is deviation from a service objective, use a dot plot centered on the objective and label the transformation.

Dual axes. Mapping alert count to a left axis and compromise loss to an independently scaled right axis lets the author manufacture apparent correlation. Use vertically aligned panels or normalize both to a clearly named baseline index. If indexed values are used, preserve raw values in labels.

Log scales. A log scale is useful when values span orders of magnitude, such as host byte counts from 100 to 100,000,000. It turns equal ratios into equal distances. It is invalid for zero and awkward for signed differences. Label powers explicitly and explain that the distance from 100 to 1,000 equals the distance from 1,000 to 10,000. For an executive ranking of absolute financial exposure, a log axis may visually understate the largest item; use it only when multiplicative comparison is the task.

A composition chart also deserves suspicion. A pie chart of alert categories can show that 60 percent are phishing, but it cannot show that total alerts tripled. A stacked area chart makes only its baseline category easy to compare. Prefer direct lines for a few categories or a heat map when category-by-time density is the actual question.

Station 3: Put Calibration on Trial

A classifier assigns probabilities, but the dashboard shows only ROC AUC. In rare-event work, a model may rank well while producing probabilities that cannot support staffing or automated action.

Use ten equal-width probability bins or, when predictions cluster, quantile bins with displayed boundaries. Within bin \(b\), calculate mean predicted probability and observed event frequency:

\[ \operatorname{ECE}=\sum_b \frac{n_b}{N}\left|\bar p_b-\bar y_b\right|. \]

pred = [0.02,0.04,0.08,0.12,0.18,0.55,0.62,0.76,0.84,0.93]
truth = [0,0,0,0,1,0,1,1,1,1]
bins = [(0,.2),(.2,.6),(.6,1.0001)]
ece=0
for lo,hi in bins:
    idx=[i for i,p in enumerate(pred) if lo<=p<hi]
    mp=sum(pred[i] for i in idx)/len(idx)
    oy=sum(truth[i] for i in idx)/len(idx)
    ece += len(idx)/len(pred)*abs(mp-oy)
    print(f"[{lo:.1f},{hi:.1f}) n={len(idx)} predicted={mp:.3f} observed={oy:.3f}")
print(f"ECE={ece:.3f}")

Exact output:

[0.0,0.2) n=5 predicted=0.088 observed=0.200
[0.2,0.6) n=1 predicted=0.550 observed=0.000
[0.6,1.0) n=4 predicted=0.787 observed=1.000
ECE=0.196

The reliability diagram plots observed rate against predicted probability with a 45-degree reference. Add bin counts below it: a point based on four cases must not look as authoritative as one based on forty thousand. ECE is a summary, not a substitute for the diagram; binning choices can hide local failure.

For discrimination under class imbalance, show the precision-recall curve and label the prevalence baseline. ROC plots can remain useful for comparing ranking across thresholds, but a low false-positive rate multiplied by millions of benign events may still overwhelm operations. Mark the deployed threshold and annotate its precision, recall, alerts per day, and confidence interval.

Station 4: Separate Drift into Questions

“The model drifted” conflates at least three displays.

  1. Input drift: did feature distributions change? For categorical device posture, show shares by category and an “unknown” category rather than dropping missing values. For a numeric feature, overlay empirical cumulative distributions or use fixed, meaningful bins.
  2. Prediction drift: did the score distribution move? Show quantiles over time, with deployment and policy changes annotated.
  3. Performance drift: conditional on matured labels, did precision, recall, calibration, or cost change? Align each metric to the event cohort date, not the date on which its delayed label arrived.

Population Stability Index is often overinterpreted. For reference distribution shares \(r_i\) and current shares \(c_i\),

\[ PSI=\sum_i(c_i-r_i)\log(c_i/r_i). \]

from math import log
reference=[.70,.20,.10]; current=[.50,.30,.20]
parts=[(c-r)*log(c/r) for r,c in zip(reference,current)]
print([round(x,4) for x in parts], round(sum(parts),4))

Expected output:

[0.0673, 0.0405, 0.0693] 0.1772

This says the binned distribution differs; it does not identify cause, harm, or a universal retraining threshold. Show category contributions so reviewers can see what moved. Fix bins from the reference interval and handle zero shares with a declared smoothing rule.

Station 5: Draw a Causal Effect Without Hiding Time

A security-key rollout begins in week 5. A before/after line for treated employees drops from 8 to 4 compromises per 100,000 logins. That does not establish an effect if the untreated group fell from 6 to 3 during the same incident lull.

The difference-in-differences estimate is

\[ (4-8)-(3-6)=-1 \]

compromise per 100,000 logins. The redesign shows treated and comparison rates across all weeks, an intervention marker, pre-period trend, and a second panel of estimated weekly treatment effects with confidence intervals. Do not plot only the aggregate pre/post bars: they conceal pre-trend failure and timing.

If rollout timing varies, an event-study display aligns units by time relative to adoption. Include leads before treatment; nonzero pre-treatment coefficients warn that parallel trends may be implausible. State whether intervals account for clustering. A visual recommendation should say “consistent with one fewer compromise per 100,000 under the stated comparison,” not “security keys prevented every observed decline.”

Accessibility Review at the Door

Color is redundant, never sole meaning. Use line style, point shape, direct labels, or texture in addition to hue. A red/green status display excludes many readers and turns uncertainty into traffic lights. Prefer a blue/orange palette with text labels such as “within range” and “outside range.”

Meet WCAG contrast requirements for text and essential graphical objects. Avoid low-contrast gridlines competing with data. Provide concise alt text that states the chart's question, principal pattern, exception, and uncertainty. Preserve reading order and data tables for keyboard and screen-reader users. Do not rely on hover for denominators or threshold values.

Example alt text: “Weekly confirmed-compromise rate rose from 1.00 to 1.20 per 100,000 eligible logins while alert rate fell from 50.00 to 16.57. Review precision rose from 2.22 to 7.78 percent. The display does not determine why compromise incidence increased.”

Studio Critique: Match Display to Audience

An investigator needs event-level drill-down and reversible filters. A model owner needs cohort-aligned calibration, drift, threshold, and latency diagnostics. An executive needs the decision, affected population, magnitude, uncertainty, and alternatives. Giving all three audiences the same wall of tiles is not consistency; it is refusal to edit.

For a decision memo, lead with a complete sentence: “Observed compromise incidence increased despite lower alert burden; hold the proposed coverage reduction until the missing segment is explained.” Follow with the smallest figure that supports the sentence, then limitations and requested action. Avoid “data storytelling” that assigns motives to an attacker or causal credit to a control without evidence.

Critique Bench: Questions Before Pixels

Before accepting a chart, conduct a five-minute verbal review. What comparison must the reader make: position, length, angle, area, color, or animation? Position on a shared scale is generally easier to compare than area or angle, which is why a sorted dot plot often beats bubbles or a pie. What data disappeared through aggregation? Which records were excluded? Does the visual encode an outcome, a proxy, or an operational activity? What action would change if the pattern reversed?

Then inspect the calculation. Confirm time zones and whether weekly buckets are calendar weeks or rolling seven-day windows. Check whether a “daily average” is the mean of tenant averages—which weights small and large tenants equally—or total events divided by total exposure. If customers have radically different sizes, show both the population-weighted rate and the distribution of tenant rates. Simpson's paradox can make the aggregate improve while every important segment worsens because the mixture changed.

Uncertainty is not limited to confidence intervals. Labels may be incomplete, identity resolution may merge people, and controls may change what becomes observable. Encode maturity with open points, hatching, or a separate recent-period band rather than extending a confident line into unresolved cohorts. Annotate known instrumentation changes on the time axis. A vertical line labeled “collector v3 deployed” often explains more than another anomaly color.

Small multiples require disciplined scales. Shared scales support comparison but can flatten a small segment; free scales expose within-segment shape but prohibit magnitude comparison. State the choice. Sorting categories by value helps ranking; preserving a stable semantic order helps repeated scanning. Do not reorder severity levels alphabetically or let a dashboard resort them each refresh.

Network diagrams deserve a special warning. A force-directed hairball converts node count into visual authority while edge overlap hides paths. Begin with the question. For reachability, show the few typed paths to the protected resource. For neighborhood review, use an ego graph with degree limits. For group comparison, an adjacency matrix or summarized edge counts may be clearer. Label edge direction and time validity; otherwise a visually connected node can imply access that never existed simultaneously.

The final review is linguistic. Titles should make claims, subtitles should define population and period, and annotations should distinguish observation from explanation. “Compromise rate increased after rollout” is descriptive. “Rollout caused compromise” is causal. A responsible display never smuggles the second sentence through the grammar of the first.

Export matters too. Review the figure at presentation size, on a grayscale printer, at 200 percent zoom, and in the document where it will actually appear. A dashboard tooltip cannot rescue a screenshot pasted into an incident report. Preserve a machine-readable table beside static exports, record the query and snapshot used to generate them, and put the generation timestamp somewhere less prominent than the event-time coverage. “Updated today” does not mean the underlying labels are current.

When animation is proposed, ask whether motion reveals transition or merely attracts attention. A scrubber can help inspect an evolving attack path; autoplaying points usually obstruct comparison and creates inaccessible content. Prefer stable views with selectable time states and a textual summary of what changed.

Interview Practicum

The Executive Demands One Light

Prompt with live evidence

“Alert volume is down 42 percent, precision is up, compromise incidence is also up, and the executive wants one red/green indicator.” Then the interviewer reveals that only 60 percent of alerts have matured labels and the product launch doubled eligible login volume.

Worked Answer

Strong response

Decline the false compression while meeting the need for a decision. Offer a headline state tied to an explicit action—“coverage review required”—plus three supporting measures: compromise rate per eligible login, alert burden per analyst hour, and reviewed-alert precision with label maturity. Recalculate all count metrics using eligible exposure. Separate mature cohorts from recent incomplete ones and show their denominators.

Use aligned small multiples, not a weighted score whose weights conceal tradeoffs. If governance insists on a status, define it from preapproved conditions: for example, red if the mature compromise-rate interval exceeds the guardrail, amber if label maturity is insufficient, and green only when outcome and operational guardrails pass. The state must carry an “insufficient evidence” condition; missing labels are not green.

Misleading executive compromises

An average of normalized metrics allows a favorable alert decline to cancel rising harm. A dual-axis chart can visually force agreement. Showing counts ignores the launch-driven exposure change. Picking red “to be safe” avoids the analytical question and eventually destroys trust in status signals.

Follow-Up Questions and Answers

Display-review follow-ups

What if compromise labels arrive sixty days late? Freeze performance panels by event cohort and maturity; use leading operational measures separately, never as if they were confirmed outcomes.

What if the executive has ten seconds? State the action and reason: “Do not reduce coverage: confirmed compromise per eligible login is higher, and 40 percent of recent labels are unresolved.”

How would you test the redesign? Give representative analysts and leaders decision tasks, measure whether they identify the correct direction and denominator, audit accessibility, and compare decisions—not aesthetic preference.

Key Takeaways

  • Chart rates against the population that could experience the event and display numerator, denominator, and uncertainty.
  • Calibration, discrimination, drift, and causal effect answer different questions and require different displays.
  • Visual geometry carries assumptions: zero baselines, log scales, binning, and dual axes are analytical decisions.
  • Accessibility requires redundant encoding, contrast, alt text, and non-hover access to essential values.
  • A decision display should preserve conflict when the evidence conflicts; a single score must not make harm disappear.
