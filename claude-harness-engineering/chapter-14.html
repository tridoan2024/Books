Chapter 14: Scheduling — CronCreate and ScheduleWakeup

"An agent that only runs when you tell it to is a fancy autocomplete. An agent that wakes up by itself is an employee."

Most tools in a harness are reactive: the model calls them in response to a user prompt. Scheduling tools invert this relationship. They let the harness reach into the future and arrange for work to happen autonomously — while the REPL is idle, while the engineer is asleep, while a release is baking. Two tools drive this capability: CronCreate for time-based recurrence, and ScheduleWakeup for dynamic loop pacing. They look similar on the surface. They solve completely different problems.

This chapter covers both tools at depth — their interfaces, behavioral contracts, failure modes, and the practical patterns that distinguish production-grade scheduling from amateur setups that fire at :00 and wonder why the whole planet is congested.

14.1 CronCreate: Five-Field Cron in Local Timezone

The Interface

export interface CronCreateInput {
  cron: string;          // "M H DoM Mon DoW" — local timezone
  prompt: string;        // prompt to enqueue at fire time
  recurring?: boolean;   // default true — fire every match until deleted/expired
  durable?: boolean;     // default false — true persists to .claude/scheduled_tasks.json
}

export interface CronCreateOutput {
  id: string;
  humanSchedule: string;
  recurring: boolean;
  durable?: boolean;
}

CronCreate schedules a prompt to be enqueued at a future time defined by a standard five-field cron expression. The field order is the POSIX standard:

M H DoM Mon DoW
│ │  │   │   └── Day of Week (0-7, 0 and 7 are Sunday)
│ │  │   └────── Month (1-12)
│ │  └────────── Day of Month (1-31)
│ └───────────── Hour (0-23)
└─────────────── Minute (0-59)

The expressions follow standard cron syntax including * (any), , (list), - (range), and / (step). A few valid examples:

"7 11 * * 1-5"    — Monday through Friday at 11:07 local time
"23 */4 * * *"    — Every 4 hours at :23 past the hour
"15 9 1 * *"      — First of every month at 9:15
"47 18 * * 5"     — Every Friday at 18:47

Timezone: All cron expressions are evaluated in the local system timezone. On a machine locked to CDT, 7 11 * * 1-5 fires at 11:07 CDT — it does not drift if the machine crosses a DST boundary because the runtime evaluates wall-clock local time, not UTC offsets. This is almost always what you want: "run at 9 AM my time" should survive daylight saving changes.

The humanSchedule Field

The output's humanSchedule is a plain-English rendering of the cron expression, produced by the harness for display in CronList output and audit logs. You cannot control it — the harness generates it. When you call CronCreate and get back:

{
  "id": "903b1a1c",
  "humanSchedule": "At 11:07 AM, Monday through Friday",
  "recurring": true,
  "durable": true
}

That humanSchedule is what the user sees when listing scheduled jobs. It is also useful for verifying your cron expression did what you intended — if your expression was 7 11 * * 1-5 and humanSchedule says "At 11:07 AM, Monday through Friday," you got it right. If it says something unexpected, debug your expression before the job fires.

When Jobs Fire

Jobs fire when the cron expression matches and the REPL is idle. That last condition is critical and non-negotiable: the harness will not interrupt a running query to fire a scheduled job. If a job is due at 11:07 and a 20-minute background scan is still running at 11:07, the job will fire when the scan completes and the REPL returns to idle.

This is correct behavior for most production use cases. You do not want a security scan starting mid-conversation and racing with an ongoing operation. The practical implication: for tight time-critical jobs, do not schedule them adjacent to other long-running operations. A job nominally scheduled for 11:07 might fire at 11:09 or 11:15 if the REPL is busy. Design your scheduled tasks to tolerate a few minutes of slippage.

14.2 One-Shot Tasks: recurring: false

By default, recurring is true. Every time the cron expression matches, the job fires. To schedule a task that runs exactly once, set recurring: false.

For one-shot tasks, your cron expression needs to specify an exact future moment — pin minute, hour, day-of-month, and month. The standard idiom is to compute the target time and write it as a fully-qualified expression:

// Fire once: July 29, 2026 at 14:23 local time
await cronCreate({
  cron: "23 14 29 7 *",
  prompt: "/deploy production — confirm all smoke tests passed",
  recurring: false,
  durable: true
});

The * in the day-of-week field is intentional — it does not matter what day July 29 falls on. Pinning DoW for one-shot tasks is redundant and can cause failures if the date/day-of-week combination is logically impossible.

Auto-Delete After Fire

When recurring: false and the job fires, the harness automatically deletes it from the job list. No cleanup required. If you set recurring: false with durable: true, the job is written to scheduled_tasks.json — and removed from that file after it fires. This is the correct behavior: a one-shot job that has already executed has no reason to persist.

The Jitter Difference for One-Shot Tasks

Recurring jobs apply jitter as a percentage of their period (covered in Section 14.5). One-shot jobs use a different rule: if the pinned time lands on a :00 or :30 boundary, the harness applies up to 90 seconds of early jitter — the job may fire between 90 seconds early and the exact scheduled time. This is the harness protecting shared infrastructure from mass-synchronized one-shot jobs.

The practical lesson: if you are scheduling a one-shot task for 14:30, it might fire at 14:28:45. Build in a 2-minute buffer before any dependent action that you expect to happen at exactly 14:30.

14.3 Durable vs. Session-Only

The durable flag is the difference between a job that survives and a job that disappears when your laptop closes.

Session-Only (durable: false, the default)

When durable is not set or is false, the job exists only in memory. It will fire normally while the harness process is running. When the process exits — REPL closed, terminal window killed, machine rebooted — the job is gone. It does not accumulate. The next time you start a session, it has no memory of any previously scheduled non-durable jobs.

Session-only jobs are appropriate for within-session pacing: "check the build status in 10 minutes," "remind me to commit before I close." They are not appropriate for anything that needs to outlast the current session.

Durable (durable: true)

When durable: true, the job is written to .claude/scheduled_tasks.json in the project directory. The harness reads this file on every startup and re-registers any unexpired jobs. The job survives session restarts, reboots, and even re-installs as long as the file is present.

Here is the scheduled_tasks.json from a real production setup — a weekday security scan:

{
  "tasks": [{
    "id": "903b1a1c",
    "cron": "7 11 * * 1-5",
    "prompt": "/sec-pr",
    "createdAt": 1780950177365,
    "lastFiredAt": 1781541420890,
    "recurring": true,
    "createdBySessionId": "cdc66b6b-8438-4dbc-8291-8a5bc240f7e4",
    "createdByPid": 61161,
    "createdByProcStart": "Wed May 27 15:34:38 2026"
  }]
}

Notice what the file stores beyond the cron expression and prompt: createdAt and lastFiredAt as epoch milliseconds, plus the creating session ID, process ID, and process start time. This metadata serves audit and recovery purposes. If a job misfires or you need to debug when it was created, these fields tell the story.

One-Shot Durable: Catch-Up on Re-Open

One-shot durable tasks have an important failure-recovery property: if the harness was closed when the job was due to fire (the machine was off, the terminal was closed), the harness surfaces the missed job for catch-up on the next launch. You get a notification that the job was due at a specific time and has not been executed — then you can choose to run it manually or skip it.

This catch-up behavior is absent for recurring durable tasks. A recurring job that was due while the harness was closed is simply skipped. The next matching moment becomes the next fire time. This is correct: a recurring status check that missed one cycle does not need to run five cycles in sequence to catch up.

What Does Not Belong in durable: true

Do not make session-utility jobs durable. "Check the build in 5 minutes" should be session-only. If you make it durable and forget to delete it, it accumulates across sessions. Over weeks, your scheduled_tasks.json fills with stale one-shot tasks that have already fired (or should have been ignored). The cleanup rule: if a job is only meaningful in the current session context, do not set durable: true.

14.4 The Off-Minute Rule: Never :00 or :30

This is a firm rule, not a preference.

The problem with :00 and :30 is not theoretical. Systems around the world schedule cron jobs at these boundaries because they are round numbers that feel natural. "Every hour" becomes 0 * * * *. "Twice daily" becomes 0 9 * * * and 0 17 * * *. The effect is a regular pulse of globally synchronized load hitting every shared resource — APIs, databases, CI systems, rate-limited endpoints — at clock-second precision. Infrastructure engineers have a name for this: the thundering herd.

For Claude Code specifically, the harness fires the enqueued prompt against the Claude API. The API has rate limits. If a significant fraction of the global user base has jobs scheduled at :00, those jobs create synchronized API load spikes. The harness implements automatic off-minute scheduling to protect against this.

The Conversion Pattern

Always convert natural-language schedule requests into off-minute cron expressions:

User saysWrongRight
"every morning around 9"0 9 * * *57 8 * * *
"hourly"0 * * * *7 * * * *
"every 6 hours"0 */6 * * *23 */6 * * *
"weekdays at noon"0 12 * * 1-543 11 * * 1-5
"first of every month"0 9 1 * *17 8 1 * *
"every 15 minutes"0,15,30,45 * * * *3,18,33,48 * * * *

The offset does not matter much — 7, 17, 23, 43, 57 are all fine. What matters is avoiding :00 and :30. Pick a number that feels arbitrary. It is.

When :00 Is Legitimate

There are genuine cases where :00 or :30 is correct:

  • The user explicitly says "at 9:00 sharp" — they are coordinating with a meeting, a deployment window, or an external event that happens at that exact time.
  • The job must interlock with another system that fires at :00 and requires your job to have already completed.
  • A human-facing notification that goes out "on the hour" because that is the convention the team uses.

In these cases, use :00 and document why. The rule is not absolute — it is a default that should be overridden only with explicit justification.

The Jitter System Works Alongside This Rule

Even with off-minute scheduling, the harness applies deterministic jitter (covered next). The two mechanisms compound: you pick an off-minute base, and jitter adds a final randomization pass. Together they prevent coordinated load even among multiple engineers using the same canonical off-minute patterns.

14.5 Seven-Day Auto-Expiry and Deterministic Jitter

Auto-Expiry

Every recurring job automatically expires after 7 days. The harness fires the job one final time at its last scheduled moment before expiry, then deletes it. This is not a soft suggestion — it is enforced by the runtime.

The practical consequences:

  1. Recurring jobs are not a set-and-forget infrastructure. They require periodic renewal.
  2. If you need a job to run indefinitely, you must re-create it before 7 days elapse. The cleanest approach is to have the job prompt itself trigger re-creation:
/sec-pr

After the scan completes, if today is Thursday or Friday, re-schedule this job for next week:
CronCreate(cron="7 11 * * 1-5", prompt="/sec-pr", recurring=true, durable=true)

This pattern — a job that reschedules itself — is robust to manual restarts and session gaps. The 7-day limit becomes a rolling renewal instead of a hard stop.

  1. One-shot jobs (recurring: false) are exempt from the 7-day expiry. They expire by firing once, regardless of when they fire.

Deterministic Jitter

The harness applies jitter to prevent synchronized load, even after you have already chosen an off-minute base. The jitter formula for recurring jobs:

jitter = min(period_seconds * 0.10, 900)

Ten percent of the job's period, capped at 15 minutes (900 seconds). The jitter is deterministic per job — it is derived from the job's ID, not a random number at fire time. This means the same job always fires at the same offset from its nominal time — you can predict it once you know the ID. But different jobs with the same nominal schedule will fire at different offsets, which is the load-spreading effect you want.

Examples of jitter in practice:

SchedulePeriodMax Jitter
7 * * * * (hourly)3600s360s (6 min)
7 */4 * * * (every 4h)14400s900s (15 min, capped)
7 11 * * 1-5 (weekdays 11:07)~86400s900s (15 min, capped)
3,18,33,48 * * * * (every 15m)900s90s

A job nominally scheduled at 11:07 might actually fire anywhere between 11:07 and 11:22, depending on its jitter offset. For monitoring jobs and scans, this range is almost always acceptable. For jobs coordinating with external systems at hard time boundaries, account for this window.

Jitter is applied as a delay from the nominal time — the job never fires early for recurring tasks. It fires at the nominal time plus some jitter offset in [0, max_jitter].

14.6 ScheduleWakeup: Dynamic Loop Pacing

ScheduleWakeup is architecturally different from CronCreate. It does not schedule a new job. It controls the pacing of an existing dynamic /loop — scheduling when the current loop iteration should resume.

The Interface

export interface ScheduleWakeupInput {
  delaySeconds?: number;  // clamped [60, 3600], required unless stop:true
  reason?: string;        // shown to user, goes to telemetry
  prompt?: string;        // /loop input to re-fire
  stop?: boolean;         // true ends the dynamic loop immediately
}

export interface ScheduleWakeupOutput {
  scheduledFor: number;          // epoch ms when wakeup is scheduled
  clampedDelaySeconds: number;   // actual delay after clamping
  wasClamped: boolean;           // true if outside [60, 3600]
  stopped?: boolean;             // true when stop:true was used
  cancelledWakeups?: number;     // how many pending wakeups were cancelled
}

The delaySeconds field is clamped to the range [60, 3600]. You cannot request a wakeup in less than 60 seconds, and you cannot request one more than an hour away. If you pass 45, you get 60. If you pass 7200, you get 3600. The wasClamped field in the output tells you whether the clamping activated.

The scheduledFor field is an epoch millisecond timestamp. You can compute the actual wall-clock fire time by converting it: new Date(output.scheduledFor).toISOString(). This is useful for logging and for surfacing the next wakeup time to the user.

ScheduleWakeup Is Only for /loop

This is the most important constraint to understand: ScheduleWakeup is exclusively for the /loop dynamic mode. It does not create a cron job. It does not persist. It does not survive session restarts. It paces a specific running autonomous loop within the current session.

If you find yourself reaching for ScheduleWakeup outside of a dynamic loop, you want CronCreate instead. The tool names reflect their purposes: "wakeup" is "wake this loop up again," not "schedule a new task."

The reason and prompt Fields

reason is a human-readable string that appears in the user interface while the loop is sleeping. It also goes to telemetry. A good reason tells the user what the loop is waiting for:

"Waiting 4 minutes — checking scan freshness before next cycle"
"Pausing 10 minutes — rate limit window resets at :45"
"Sleeping 5 minutes — build in progress, next poll at 14:30"

prompt is what gets fed back into the /loop when the wakeup fires. If omitted, the loop resumes with its original prompt. If provided, it overrides the prompt for that cycle. This allows the loop to pass state forward:

"Cycle 7 — last scan clean. Run /sec-pr and report findings."

Using prompt for state forwarding is more robust than relying on conversation context alone, because the loop's conversation may be compacted across long-running sessions.

14.7 Cache-Aware Delays: The 270s vs 300s Decision

This section is about money and latency, and the numbers matter precisely.

The Prompt Cache

The Claude API caches the system prompt (and large stable conversation prefixes) for a 5-minute TTL. While the cache is warm, repeated API calls within the same session pay a small fraction of the normal token cost for the cached portion. When the cache expires, the next call pays full price to re-read and re-tokenize all the context.

The TTL is 5 minutes, which is 300 seconds.

The 270s Rule

If your loop's delay is under 270 seconds, the cache remains warm for the next cycle. When the wakeup fires and the loop makes its next API call, the system prompt and stable context are already cached. You pay a cache-read rate on that context — roughly 10% of the normal input token rate. For a session with a 50k-token context window, the difference is significant across dozens of loop cycles.

Cache-warm call:  50,000 tokens × $0.003/1k (cached) = $0.15
Cache-cold call:  50,000 tokens × $0.030/1k (input)  = $1.50

Ten times more expensive. Per cycle. For a loop running every 5 minutes for 8 hours (96 cycles), the cost difference between cache-warm and cache-cold is $0.15 × 96 = $14.40 vs $1.50 × 96 = $144.00. This is not a rounding error.

Never Pick 300 Seconds

The worst possible delay is exactly 300 seconds — the cache TTL. Here is why:

  • At exactly 300s, the cache is expiring. Your call may or may not be warm depending on API-side timing jitter.
  • You get all the cost of a cache miss (unpredictable) without the benefit of amortization.
  • You have not bought yourself any meaningful additional sleeping time compared to 270s.

The three useful regions are:

Delay RangeCache StatusWhen to Use
60–270sWarm (5-min TTL safe)High-frequency monitoring, tight polling, build status checks
271–299sUncertain (TTL boundary)Avoid — worst of both worlds
300–3600sCold (accept the miss)Low-frequency checks where amortizing a cold read is fine; the miss cost is spread over a long idle period

The clean rule: stay under 270s to stay warm, or use 1200s+ to amortize the cold read. Never pick 300s.

Practical Delay Selection

Ask two questions:

  1. How quickly does this loop need to respond to changes?
  2. How large is my context window?

A tight monitoring loop that needs to catch issues within 2 minutes should use 120s. The cache stays warm, each cycle is cheap.

A background audit loop that runs once every 20 minutes should use 1200s. The cache is cold, but the cost of one cold read amortized over 20 minutes is acceptable — especially since the loop does significant work per cycle and the cold-read cost is a small fraction of the total per-cycle cost.

// Tight monitoring — stay warm
await scheduleWakeup({
  delaySeconds: 120,
  reason: "Checking build status every 2 minutes",
  prompt: "Check CI for failures. If clean, ScheduleWakeup(120). If failing, alert and stop."
});

// Background audit — accept cold read
await scheduleWakeup({
  delaySeconds: 1200,
  reason: "Next security scan in 20 minutes",
  prompt: "Run full sec-pr scan. ScheduleWakeup(1200) after completion."
});

// Never do this
await scheduleWakeup({
  delaySeconds: 300,   // Cache-miss roulette, no upside
  reason: "..."
});

14.8 The <<autonomous-loop-dynamic>> Sentinel

When building a fully autonomous loop — one that manages its own pacing and stop conditions without user interaction — the harness provides a special sentinel value for the /loop prompt:

<<autonomous-loop-dynamic>>

This sentinel tells the loop runtime that pacing is entirely self-managed. The loop will not apply any default interval between cycles. Instead, every iteration must call ScheduleWakeup to schedule the next cycle, or call ScheduleWakeup with stop: true to end the loop.

Without the sentinel, /loop applies a default rest interval between iterations. The sentinel disables this default and hands full control to the model. The model becomes responsible for deciding when to wake up next and when to stop.

Why This Matters

Consider two architectures:

Passive loop (without sentinel):

/loop --interval 300 "check for new PRs and scan them"

The harness fires the prompt every 300 seconds. The model has no control over pacing. It cannot sleep longer when there is nothing to scan. It cannot wake up sooner when it detects incoming work.

Active loop (with sentinel):

/loop "<<autonomous-loop-dynamic>> Monitor PRs and scan on arrival."

The model decides. If there is nothing to scan, it calls ScheduleWakeup(1200) and sleeps 20 minutes. If a batch of PRs arrives, it scans them all and then calls ScheduleWakeup(120) to poll again in 2 minutes. When the work is done for the day, it calls ScheduleWakeup(stop: true) to terminate.

The dynamic loop is adaptive. The passive loop is a blunt instrument.

Sentinel Syntax

The sentinel must appear verbatim at the start of the loop prompt. The harness detects it at prompt evaluation time, strips it from the model's visible prompt, and configures the loop runtime accordingly. The model never sees <<autonomous-loop-dynamic>> in its context — it sees only the rest of the prompt.

This means the model must be instructed to use ScheduleWakeup explicitly in the accompanying prompt text. The sentinel enables the capability; the prompt instructions tell the model to use it.

14.9 stop: true — Ending the Dynamic Loop

A dynamic loop with no exit condition is a resource leak and a cost explosion. Every loop needs a defined stop condition.

The Stop Call

await scheduleWakeup({
  stop: true
  // reason is optional but recommended for logging
});

When stop: true is passed, delaySeconds is ignored. The loop terminates immediately. Any pending wakeups for this loop are cancelled. The output confirms termination:

{
  "scheduledFor": 0,
  "clampedDelaySeconds": 0,
  "wasClamped": false,
  "stopped": true,
  "cancelledWakeups": 1
}

cancelledWakeups tells you how many wakeups were pending when stop was called. In a well-designed loop, this should usually be 0 or 1 — you call stop after completing work, before scheduling the next wakeup, or you schedule the wakeup and then realize you should stop and it cancels the pending one.

Stop Conditions to Design For

A robust dynamic loop defines stop conditions explicitly in its prompt. Common patterns:

Time-bounded: "Run until 18:00 CDT, then stop."

If current time is past 18:00 CDT, call ScheduleWakeup(stop=true) instead of scheduling next cycle.

Work-completed: "Scan all open PRs. Stop when the queue is empty."

After each cycle, check if any un-scanned PRs remain. If queue is empty, call ScheduleWakeup(stop=true).

Error threshold: "Stop if three consecutive cycles fail."

Track consecutive failures in the forwarded prompt. If failure_count >= 3, ScheduleWakeup(stop=true) and alert.

Daily limit: "Run at most 20 cycles per day."

Track cycle_count in the forwarded prompt. If cycle_count >= 20, ScheduleWakeup(stop=true).

The forwarded prompt field is the primary mechanism for carrying state across cycles. Keep state minimal — a few key-value pairs. The prompt field is not a database.

14.10 Lab: Build an Autonomous Monitoring Daemon

This lab builds a complete, production-ready monitoring daemon: a scheduled wakeup that fires a dynamic scan loop during business hours and goes quiet at night.

Goal

Scan a codebase for new security findings every 2 minutes during working hours (8 AM–6 PM, weekdays). Outside working hours, pause without consuming API credits. Automatically resume the next morning. Run for 7 days, then renew itself.

Architecture

flowchart TD
    A([CronCreate: Weekdays 07:57]) -->|fires| B[Dynamic Loop Start]
    B --> C{Within 08:00-18:00?}
    C -->|yes| D[Run Security Scan]
    D --> E{Findings?}
    E -->|yes| F[Post Alert to ADO]
    E -->|no| G[Log Clean Cycle]
    F --> H[ScheduleWakeup 120s]
    G --> H
    H --> C
    C -->|no, past 18:00| I[ScheduleWakeup stop=true]
    I --> J([Loop Ends — Tomorrow's Cron Restarts It])

The key insight: a CronCreate starts the loop each morning. The loop runs using ScheduleWakeup during the day. When the loop detects it is past 18:00, it stops itself. Tomorrow's cron fires again to restart.

This separation of concerns is deliberate: CronCreate handles the "when to start" problem. ScheduleWakeup handles the "how fast to run" problem. Neither tool does the other's job.

Step 1: Register the Daily Trigger

const trigger = await cronCreate({
  cron: "57 7 * * 1-5",          // 07:57 local, weekdays
  prompt: `<<autonomous-loop-dynamic>>
You are a security monitoring daemon. Today's date is provided in the system context.

At the start of each cycle:
1. Check the current local time. If it is past 18:00, call ScheduleWakeup(stop=true) and stop.
2. Run: Bash("python sec_scanner.py --changed-since 2min --format json")
3. Parse the JSON output. If any findings have severity HIGH or CRITICAL, post a comment to ADO PR.
4. Call ScheduleWakeup(delaySeconds=120, reason="Next scan cycle in 2 minutes", prompt="<<autonomous-loop-dynamic>> [continue monitoring]")

On the first cycle of the day, also check if the CronCreate job for tomorrow needs renewal.
If today is Wednesday or Thursday (job created Monday expires Sunday), re-register:
CronCreate(cron="57 7 * * 1-5", prompt="[same prompt]", recurring=true, durable=true)
`,
  recurring: true,
  durable: true
});

console.log(`Daemon registered: ${trigger.id}`);
console.log(`Schedule: ${trigger.humanSchedule}`);
// Daemon registered: 903b1a1c
// Schedule: At 07:57 AM, Monday through Friday

Step 2: Verify the Registration

const jobs = await cronList({});
const daemon = jobs.jobs.find(j => j.id === trigger.id);

if (!daemon) {
  throw new Error("Daemon registration failed — not found in CronList");
}

console.log(`Verified: ${daemon.id} — ${daemon.humanSchedule}`);
console.log(`Prompt length: ${daemon.prompt.length} chars`);
console.log(`Durable: ${daemon.durable}`);

Always verify after registering. CronCreate can succeed but the job can fail to persist to scheduled_tasks.json if the file is locked or the directory is not writable. The verification step catches this before the first fire time.

Step 3: The Scan Loop Implementation

The prompt in Step 1 encodes the loop logic inline. That is intentional — the prompt is the program. For complex loops, break the logic into a shell script and invoke it:

#!/usr/bin/env bash
# .claude/scripts/security-scan-cycle.sh
set -euo pipefail

readonly CURRENT_HOUR
CURRENT_HOUR=$(date '+%H')

# Stop condition: past 18:00
if [[ "${CURRENT_HOUR}" -ge 18 ]]; then
    echo "STOP: Past 18:00, stopping loop"
    exit 0
fi

# Run scan
readonly FINDINGS
FINDINGS=$(python sec_scanner.py --changed-since 2min --format json 2>/dev/null)

readonly CRITICAL_COUNT
CRITICAL_COUNT=$(echo "${FINDINGS}" | jq '[.[] | select(.severity | IN("HIGH", "CRITICAL"))] | length')

if [[ "${CRITICAL_COUNT}" -gt 0 ]]; then
    echo "ALERT: ${CRITICAL_COUNT} critical/high findings"
    echo "${FINDINGS}" | jq '.[] | select(.severity | IN("HIGH", "CRITICAL"))'
else
    echo "CLEAN: No critical findings this cycle"
fi

Then the prompt becomes simpler:

<<autonomous-loop-dynamic>>
Run: Bash(".claude/scripts/security-scan-cycle.sh")

If the output starts with "STOP:", call ScheduleWakeup(stop=true).
If the output starts with "ALERT:", post the findings to ADO and call ScheduleWakeup(120).
If the output starts with "CLEAN:", call ScheduleWakeup(120).

Externalizing logic to a shell script keeps the prompt short, testable, and diffable in git. The model's job becomes parsing the script's structured output and deciding what to do — not reimplementing the detection logic on every cycle.

Step 4: Self-Renewal Logic

The 7-day expiry means the daemon needs to re-register itself. The cleanest approach is to do this check on the first cycle of the week:

#!/usr/bin/env bash
# .claude/scripts/check-daemon-renewal.sh
set -euo pipefail

readonly DAEMON_ID="903b1a1c"
readonly TODAY_DOW
TODAY_DOW=$(date '+%u')  # 1=Monday, 5=Friday

# Re-register on Monday (DOW=1) to start fresh 7-day window
if [[ "${TODAY_DOW}" -eq 1 ]]; then
    echo "RENEW: Monday — delete and re-register daemon"
    echo "DELETE_ID: ${DAEMON_ID}"
else
    echo "OK: No renewal needed today"
fi

On Monday, the loop prompt detects "RENEW:", calls CronDelete with the current ID, then calls CronCreate with the same parameters to get a fresh 7-day window. The new job ID is stored in the forwarded prompt for the next renewal cycle.

Step 5: Verify the Full System

After setting up the daemon, run three verification checks:

Check 1 — List confirms registration:

CronList → should show exactly one job with id=903b1a1c, cron="57 7 * * 1-5"

Check 2 — Manual dry run:

Run the scan script manually: Bash(".claude/scripts/security-scan-cycle.sh")
Confirm it produces "CLEAN:" or "ALERT:" output, not an error.

Check 3 — Cache-timing audit:

During a live loop, inspect ScheduleWakeupOutput.clampedDelaySeconds.
Confirm it is consistently 120, not 300. wasClamped should be false.

If wasClamped: true appears, something in the loop prompt is generating a delay outside [60, 3600]. Track it down — it means your loop is not pacing as intended.

What This Daemon Demonstrates

  • CronCreate handles time-based startup. It does not care about loop dynamics.
  • ScheduleWakeup handles loop pacing. It does not persist across sessions.
  • Durable + self-renewal is the pattern for indefinite recurrence beyond the 7-day limit.
  • Cache-warm delays (120s, well under 270s) keep per-cycle costs low.
  • The sentinel enables adaptive pacing — the loop stops itself when the day ends.
  • External scripts keep prompts short and logic testable.

Summary

Two tools, two problems.

CronCreate is the harness's alarm clock. You give it a five-field cron expression, a prompt, and optional durability. It fires the prompt when the expression matches and the REPL is idle. Use recurring: false for one-shots. Use durable: true for anything that needs to outlast the current session. Never schedule at :00 or :30 unless coordinating with an external boundary. Expect jitter of up to 10% of the period, capped at 15 minutes. Plan for 7-day expiry in any long-running setup.

ScheduleWakeup is the loop's throttle. It only makes sense inside a /loop running with <<autonomous-loop-dynamic>>. It controls when the next iteration runs. Use delays under 270s to stay cache-warm. Use 1200s+ to amortize a cold read. Never use 300s. Always define a stop condition — call ScheduleWakeup(stop: true) when the work is done.

The combination — a CronCreate that starts a dynamic loop each morning, a loop that uses ScheduleWakeup for intraday pacing, and self-renewal logic to survive the 7-day window — is the production pattern for autonomous monitoring daemons. It is not complicated once you understand which tool solves which sub-problem.
